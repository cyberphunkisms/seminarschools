# Marcel Detienne, *Comparing the Incomparable*: Foreword and Chapter 1 research notes

## Scope, citation key, and evidentiary status

These notes cover the Foreword, “Toward a Constructive Comparativism: Between Historians and Anthropologists,” printed pp. ix–xv, and Chapter 1, “Should an Anthropologist Happen to Meet a Historian,” printed pp. 1–21. The page map is: printed ix–xv = PDF pp. 6–12; printed 1–21 = PDF pp. 16–36. All claims in the first four substantive sections below are Detienne’s claims, descriptions, examples, or self-qualifications unless explicitly marked as analysis. Historical terms such as “savages,” “primitives,” “Kaffirs,” “Laps,” and peoples “without civilization/writing/history” are retained only where needed to record the classifications Detienne quotes or criticizes.

## I. Foreword: complete argument and example ledger

- **F01 — Comparison is a basic intellectual operation.** Detienne begins from Bernardin de Saint-Pierre’s observation that the mind is strongly disposed to compare. Consumer appraisal and criticism already operate by comparison, while discernment consists in establishing analogies, sensing reasons, and sketching an argument. Comparison therefore links perception, evaluation, and reasoning rather than belonging solely to a formal scholarly method. (Detienne, p. ix; PDF p. 6.)

- **F02 — Ordinary comparison slides quickly into valuation.** Detienne describes a “grammatical capillarity” through which appreciation becomes calculation, superficial assessment, and then a value judgment. The maxim “One can only compare that which is comparable” already assumes an initial selection of eligible objects and can exclude a comparison before anyone has constructed its terms. (Detienne, p. ix; PDF p. 6.)

- **F03 — Comparability should be produced through inquiry, not presumed as an intrinsic resemblance.** The decision that something is immediately “comparable” is itself a concealed judgment. The methodological problem is therefore how to construct a comparable, rather than how to locate objects that common sense has already declared alike. (Detienne, p. ix; PDF p. 6.)

- **F04 — Comparative anatomy rebuts the common maxim.** Anatomists do not morally rank organs before comparing them across the range of animal species. Their object is generated by a research operation that selects relations and features. (Detienne, p. ix; PDF p. 6.)

- **F05 — Comparative grammar supplies a second rebuttal.** A linguist comparing Caucasian or Indo-European languages may select morphology, phonetics, or vocabulary as the operative dimension. No pre-existing total resemblance is required, and saying that only already-comparable languages may be compared would be absurd. (Detienne, p. ix; PDF p. 6.)

- **F06 — The maxim is disciplinary common sense among historians.** Detienne says reputable historians have repeated it since the 1920s. It confines inquiry to an immediately familiar circle and thus makes the dominant opinion of one society, and the discipline’s confidence in its own “incomparables,” govern the horizon of possible knowledge. He contrasts this with anthropology, in which the maxim would sound incongruous even when uttered by a territorial fieldworker. (Detienne, pp. ix–x; PDF pp. 6–7.)

- **F07 — Disciplinary common sense is historically variable.** What appears self-evident within history does not appear so within anthropology. Detienne therefore treats French intellectual life over roughly a century as a particularly revealing case, rather than treating disciplinary habits as universal epistemic necessities. (Detienne, p. x; PDF p. 7.)

- **F08 — Anthropology and historical science were organized around opposed dispositions.** Comparative work is constitutive of anthropological scholarship, whereas historical science receives comparison as an unexpected and suspicious intrusion, especially when it is constructive, experimental, and jointly undertaken by historians and anthropologists. (Detienne, p. x; PDF p. 7.)

- **F09 — Tylor’s early anthropology compared relations, not isolated likenesses.** From the nineteenth-century “science of civilizations” onward, anthropologists compared sets of relations that had first been detected and analyzed within each culture. Detienne’s model therefore requires intracultural analysis before cross-cultural construction. (Detienne, p. x; PDF p. 7.)

- **F10 — French historical science made the Nation its privileged incomparable.** While ethnology developed in Britain and the United States, French historians treated their own Nation as incommensurable. Detienne argues that history became both a science and a nationalist science, thereby contracting a domain of human societies that should belong jointly to history and anthropology. (Detienne, p. x; PDF p. 7.)

- **F11 — Nationalist history was an institution of identity production.** After 1870, historians on both banks of the Rhine helped construct a “stronghold of Incomparability.” Historical science was tasked with forging racial and national identity through earth, blood, and inherited greatness. Societies lacking civilization or writing were excluded, while comparisons between rival nations took the form “we have / they have,” with “we” presumptively receiving the greater share. (Detienne, p. x; PDF p. 7.)

- **F12 — Exclusion from comparison was reinforced by exclusion from academic positions.** Detienne’s violent image that passing comparativists were “shot on sight” is followed by an institutional claim: comparativists, ethnographers, administrators, and travelers were few and denied professorial chairs or even minor university seats. Intellectual boundaries were therefore maintained through employment and status as well as doctrine. (Detienne, pp. x–xi; PDF pp. 7–8.)

- **F13 — The book is a plea and manifesto for collaborative constructive comparativism.** Detienne wants to demonstrate concretely that historians and anthropologists need to work together. Their work should coin new categories of common sense and new comparabilities, because comparabilities are never immediate givens. (Detienne, p. xi; PDF p. 8.)

- **F14 — Constructive comparativism is expressly distinguished from typology and morphology.** Its aim is not to sort whole societies into fixed types or establish a static morphology. Its operative categories arise experimentally from problems and relations. (Detienne, p. xi; PDF p. 8.)

- **F15 — Area specialists can collaborate without surrendering expertise.** Detienne imagines medieval historians, Sinologists, and Indianists working with anthropologists. His sharper question concerns Hellenists, because elite Hellenism had allowed value judgment and celebration of Greek incommensurability to replace open comparison. (Detienne, p. xi; PDF p. 8.)

- **F16 — Greece was historically comparative before it was sealed off as incomparable.** Since the sixteenth century, Greece and America had been put into dialogue because their fables and customs seemed similar. The discovery of the Americas reopened questions about Greek beginnings, and early anthropologists wanted Greece and other ancient societies included alongside every other civilization. (Detienne, p. xi; PDF p. 8.)

- **F17 — Nationalism converted Greece into exclusive Western inheritance.** Detienne says nationalists declared Greece incomparable so that they could claim the legacy of Plato, Homer, and the West. Greek exceptionalism is thus presented as a political-academic construction rather than the result of comparison. (Detienne, p. xi; PDF p. 8.)

- **F18 — The anthropological turn in French history often produced local anthropology without comparison.** Following the French reception of social anthropology through Claude Lévi-Strauss, historians of antiquity and the Middle Ages advertised “historical anthropology” in the 1970s. Yet they commonly studied a culture or topic without conducting comparative work. (Detienne, pp. xi–xii; PDF pp. 8–9.)

- **F19 — Finley and Vernant exemplify an initial opening followed by disciplinary retreat.** Detienne watched Moses I. Finley and Jean-Pierre Vernant set societies distant in time and space into perspective, then later return to comparing Greeks with Greeks. He raises two possible mechanisms: the enclosure of “cultural specificities” and the career incentives attached to positions, advantages, and honors. Both possibilities are posed as questions rather than established motives. (Detienne, p. xii; PDF p. 9.)

- **F20 — Local anthropologies can discover new subjects while preserving territorial closure.** Studies of the Greek symposium or Roman kinship may open useful topics, yet an “anthropology of Greece” that never leaves Greek territory can convert anthropology into a label for intensified local specialization. Marc Bloch’s limited proposal to compare contemporary border regions in medieval Europe still appears innovative in this environment after seventy years. (Detienne, p. xii; PDF p. 9.)

- **F21 — Greece should operate experimentally, not serve as an exalted norm.** In “Doing anthropology with the Greeks,” Detienne calls Greeks “excellent operators.” The phrase means that Greek material can be used to generate and test questions across cases, including the intellectual effects of writing in Egypt, China, Africa, and Greece, or the constitution of political places through politico-religious, theologico-political, and politico-ritual configurations in ancient, archaic, and preindustrial societies. (Detienne, p. xii; PDF p. 9.)

- **F22 — The 1992 Collège de France chair controversy shows boundary enforcement in action.** Yves Bonnefoy proposed a chair in the comparative anthropology of ancient Greece, and the proposal passed by only two votes. Academic Hellenists immediately denounced the “collusion” between Greece and comparativism through the refrain “The Greeks are not like others.” (Detienne, pp. xii–xiii; PDF pp. 9–10.)

- **F23 — Discipline and nation mutually reward enclosure.** Comparing Greeks only with Greeks satisfies historians attached to the Nation and to divisions among disciplines. Detienne connects these divisions to institutional honors, professional recognition, decorations, academies, and ennoblement; the reward comes at the cost of a “hardly noticed dereliction of duty.” (Detienne, p. xiii; PDF p. 10.)

- **F24 — Research program 1: construct “place,” “site,” and “territory” comparatively.** With five or six societies in view, researchers can identify disparities and ask what it means to found something, trace a path, have roots, or turn a place into a nonplace. The comparison does not begin from a universal definition of territory; it constructs variables out of divergent practices. (Detienne, p. xiii; PDF p. 10.)

- **F25 — “The Earth and the Dead” links territory to nationalist and autochthonous imaginaries.** Detienne places the period from Maurice Barrès to Vichy and the new “pedigree Frenchman” at one temporal end; at another, he sets the “pure autochthonous Athenian” beside Amerindian peoples said to remove traces of their presence in places that might otherwise look territorial. The deliberate disparity is meant to disclose different operations of rootedness, autochthony, and territorial marking. (Detienne, p. xiii; PDF p. 10.)

- **F26 — Research program 2: compare regimes of historicity.** Japan, the Vedic world, Israel, and Roman pontiffs organize “the past,” change, and purported built-in knowledge of the past differently. A culture organized around divination need not make historicity from the same elements as one that values rupture and change. (Detienne, p. xiii; PDF p. 10.)

- **F27 — Research program 3: compare assembly practices rather than presuppose the State.** Detienne proposes observing ways people deliberate over common affairs in southern Ethiopia, fifteenth-century Cossack foundations, and Italian communes of the eleventh through thirteenth centuries. These are concrete practices that shape public space and a politics whose autonomy is still unsettled. (Detienne, pp. xiii–xiv; PDF pp. 10–11.)

- **F28 — Political beginnings are plural.** A democratic form invented in the Ethiopian mountains dislodges the canonical route that runs from Periclean Athens to the French Revolution of 1789. Constructive comparison recovers societies as sites of innovation and allows analysts to isolate, reduce, or focus variables of a similar nature without treating one lineage as the universal road. (Detienne, p. xiv; PDF p. 11.)

- **F29 — Research program 4: compare polytheistic systems as configurations of powers.** India, Japan, Africa, Egypt, Mesopotamia, Greece, and the Aztec world contain tightly connected powers whose practices reach across social activity and support mythic narratives. A deity, genius, or power should first be defined through the positions it occupies in a system rather than through an isolated character portrait. (Detienne, p. xiv; PDF p. 11.)

- **F30 — Polytheistic comparison uses controlled manipulation.** The analyst temporarily sets aside each deity’s individual traits, with an explicit promise to return to them later, and applies selected objects, gestures, and situations as “litmus papers.” Established divine couples and groups can be tested alongside powers that combine only in the experiment. (Detienne, p. xiv; PDF p. 11.)

- **F31 — The desired result is an informative reaction.** By bringing a power alone or in combination into relation with concrete objects, significant gestures, and situations from a polytheistic culture, the analyst may reveal an unnoticed aspect, unexpected angle, or concealed property. This is what makes the comparison experimental. (Detienne, pp. xiv–xv; PDF pp. 11–12.)

- **F32 — “Comparables cannot be constructed without experimentation.”** Detienne treats this as the core maxim of his alternative. He urges researchers not to fear upsetting chronological order or inherited historical organization when unraveling and reassembling the constituent elements of intellectual operations. (Detienne, pp. xiv–xv; PDF pp. 11–12.)

## II. Chapter 1: complete argument, example, and historical-episode ledger

### A. Renaissance “perfect history” and the invention of an open comparative field

- **C01 — The comparativist’s “impulsive eye” is a methodological disposition.** It should be lively and amused, whereas Detienne caricatures the national historian’s heavy-lidded fixed stare as suited to investigating identities such as the Tupis or English closing ranks against Scots, Irish, or French. The contrast joins epistemic style to the political form of the object being studied. (Detienne, p. 1; PDF p. 16.)

- **C02 — Sixteenth-century “perfect history” is presented as an adventurous counter-tradition.** Detienne associates it with Montaigne, explicitly described as a skeptic “with no formal qualifications,” and with humanists imaginatively compared to the young Rimbaud and disrespectful Lautréamont. Their lack of reverence allowed them to perceive ancient polymorphism and Greek-Roman interbreeding rather than an immaculate “Grandeur of Antiquity.” (Detienne, p. 1; PDF p. 16.)

- **C03 — Encounter with the Americas disrupted inherited scales of similarity.** New human communities and previously unheard-of things made established criteria of likeness unstable. This destabilization, rather than a recovered Golden Age, forms the background for a “new history.” (Detienne, p. 1; PDF p. 16.)

- **C04 — Antiquarians supply a model of indiscriminate curiosity.** Detienne portrays them as obsessive half-poets and half-inquirers who research anything and everything. The Renaissance historians convert this heterogeneous erudition into an expansive historical practice. (Detienne, pp. 1–2; PDF pp. 16–17.)

- **C05 — Bodin, La Popelinière, and Pasquier were legally trained intellectuals.** Their Parlementary situation gave them leisure and reflection, with only occasional, tactful, and ineffective remonstrances to the king as a public duty. Detienne uses their legal training and institutional location to explain a scholarship concerned with philology, customs, and institutions. (Detienne, p. 2; PDF p. 17.)

- **C06 — Their field mixed old and new cultural materials.** Italian provinces, Attica, Arcadia, feudal kingdoms, gold, spices, reports about unfamiliar peoples, plants, and animals entered one domain of mores and customs. American Indian civil forms could be considered beside the “Gallic Republic” and political organizations described by Plutarch. (Detienne, p. 2; PDF p. 17.)

- **C07 — The new history refused the model of a dead past burdening the living.** Past and present remained mobile and mutually intoxicating; gods, ancient worlds, and new worlds coexisted in the same inquiry. (Detienne, p. 2; PDF p. 17.)

- **C08 — The historian-lawyers attached history to freedom, state debate, and comprehensive subject matter.** Pasquier’s group held that all people are born free, debated affairs of State, and said history should be general with respect to notable human and natural things. Detienne presents this as a secular break from royal historiography and providential history. (Detienne, pp. 2–3; PDF pp. 17–18.)

- **C09 — Bodin’s “philosophistoricus” has an anthropological mission.** This historian should explain humanity through customs, successive forms of communal life, and the capacity to make cultural worlds. Universal history here means a plural, utopian view of civilizations, not Catholic universal history. (Detienne, p. 3; PDF p. 18.)

- **C10 — La Popelinière’s “perfect history” aims at a “representation of everything.”** The aspiration is deliberately total, joining comprehensive historical judgment to travel, observation, and ethnological encounter. (Detienne, p. 3; PDF p. 18.)

- **C11 — The unrealized 1604 voyage makes observation a condition of judgment.** In a letter to Scaliger, Joseph Justus de la Scala, discussed through George Huppert, La Popelinière says travel and careful observation of foreign lands would “solidify” historical judgment. He proposes America and Africa, imagines an agreement with the Dutch, and describes the goal as a fair and total understanding of people “both within and without.” (Detienne, p. 3; PDF p. 18.)

- **C12 — Failed execution does not erase the epistemic proposal.** The ship never sailed, and La Popelinière consoled himself with the worthiness of the desire and attempt. Detienne treats him as an unusually early historian who wanted to become an ethnologist in order to know unfamiliar communities. (Detienne, pp. 3–4; PDF pp. 18–19.)

- **C13 — Detienne explicitly relaxes strict genealogical proof.** He says he is “not too bothered” whether this Renaissance beginning satisfies the strictest criteria of genealogy. He invokes it as an imaginative inaugural scene for the later project of historians and anthropologists working together. (Detienne, p. 4; PDF p. 19.)

- **C14 — Religious fragmentation forced comparison and criticism.** The Reformation’s multiplication of sects and heresies obliged Europeans to compare beliefs, helping clear a path toward criticism of inherited traditions. Detienne’s images of St. Peter’s roof and shattered Catholic glass emphasize institutional rupture as an epistemic opening. (Detienne, p. 4; PDF p. 19.)

- **C15 — La Popelinière’s 1581 history models distance from one’s own camp.** Although a Huguenot writing on the French Religious Wars that had raged since 1550, he positioned himself at enough distance to be condemned for “errors” by Huguenot jurisdiction in Paris. Detienne uses the episode to show that comparative attention to forms of belief can unsettle internal orthodoxy. (Detienne, p. 4; PDF p. 19.)

- **C16 — Criticism and experimentation were joined in Renaissance inquiry.** Detienne endorses, through Claude Lévi-Strauss, the claim that people began to treat humanity concretely and “experiment upon themselves.” Self-comparison and comparison of others are presented as connected operations. (Detienne, p. 4; PDF p. 19.)

- **C17 — The Castilian commissions expose the violent side of experimentation.** Clerical commissions sent by the Crown of Castile asked whether Indigenous Americans were humans with immortal souls, animals, or marginal beings below resident aliens and neighboring peoples. Their inquiries could involve more physical experiments than “perfect history,” yet Detienne places both within a shared drive to experiment on self and other, near and far, living and dead. (Detienne, pp. 4–5; PDF pp. 19–20.)

### B. Greeks and Americans, comparative anthropology, and its capture by hierarchy

- **C18 — Early comparison crossed professions within a shared public space.** Legal scholars, Jesuits, Huguenots, and academicians used different strategies and achieved uneven results, but their inquiries into societies and customs belonged to one arena of debate and investigation. (Detienne, p. 5; PDF p. 20.)

- **C19 — The Society of Observers of Mankind extended the project without securing disciplinary status.** The episode distinguishes an intellectual practice from its later authorization as a learned discipline. A practice can precede its chair, curriculum, and recognized profession. (Detienne, p. 5; PDF p. 20.)

- **C20 — Anthropology’s institutionalization came late but comparative.** A mid-nineteenth-century chair in ethnographic studies or Tylor’s “Science of Civilization” followed the Science of Language and roughly coincided with the Science of History. Unlike historical science, anthropology was comparative at birth. (Detienne, p. 5; PDF p. 20.)

- **C21 — Anthropology’s first comparison objects were broad and relational.** It compared civil systems, then marriage customs and mythic stories. British first-generation anthropologists Edward B. Tylor, Lewis H. Morgan, and Andrew Lang did not exclude antiquity. (Detienne, p. 5; PDF p. 20.)

- **C22 — Classical sources were ordinary equipment for early anthropologists.** Homer, Greek tragedy, Pausanias, Herodotus, Plutarch, Hyginus, the *Library* attributed to Apollodorus, and Aelian were inherited components of their education rather than a novel interdisciplinary import. Detienne’s “family silver” image implies both availability and elite inheritance. (Detienne, pp. 5–6; PDF pp. 20–21.)

- **C23 — Fontenelle and Lafitau made Greek-American comparison explicit around 1724.** Fontenelle’s “On the Origin of Fables” and Lafitau’s comparison of American customs with earliest antiquity formed a kind of comparative ethnology centered on the intellectual activity represented by fables and mythologies. (Detienne, p. 6; PDF p. 21.)

- **C24 — The comparison generated conceptual questions rather than merely recording resemblance.** It asked what fables are, how religion differs from mythology, and why Europeans elevate religion while failing to recognize comparable phenomena in others. (Detienne, p. 6; PDF p. 21.)

- **C25 — Lafitau compared configurations of practice.** Sacrifice, abstinence, and initiation on both sides formed “a set of duties,” which he interpreted as a common civil religion extending between Ancient and New Worlds. This is an early example of comparing organized relations among gestures rather than isolated motifs. (Detienne, p. 6; PDF p. 21.)

- **C26 — Lafitau’s comparison remained governed by Christian genealogy.** He explained Greek-Iroquois conformity through the religion of the earliest Gentiles, a holy religion preceding Christianity and originating in Adam’s world. Fables became carnal ideas produced by passion and ignorance as that civil religion decayed. Comparison therefore widened the evidence while keeping Christian sacred history as its explanatory frame. (Detienne, p. 6; PDF p. 21.)

- **C27 — Fontenelle substituted an evolutionary hierarchy for sacred genealogy.** He treated Greek, “Kaffir,” Iroquois, and Lapp fables as evidence of the same weak or “imbecile” thought of humanity’s infancy: ignorant yet curious attempts to explain phenomena. The danger, for him, was the conversion of fables into religion. (Detienne, pp. 6–7; PDF pp. 21–22.)

- **C28 — The Greek-Iroquois pair became a laboratory for basic categories.** For Tylor, Morgan, and the emerging field, it drove questions about religion, the mode of thought carried by myths, and the transition to reason, philosophy, and science. (Detienne, p. 7; PDF p. 22.)

- **C29 — Tylor’s synthesis provisionally weakened Greek superiority.** *Primitive Culture* considered so-called savage groups, ancient societies, and modern Western societies within one field, attending to distinctive features of the last rather than treating superiority as the sole premise. (Detienne, p. 7; PDF p. 22.)

- **C30 — A common historian-anthropologist archive appeared possible.** New cultures and ancient cultures could be documented, classified, and compared within an evolutionist sequence from simple to complex. Detienne recognizes this as a possible new horizon of intelligibility before describing its corruption. (Detienne, p. 7; PDF p. 22.)

- **C31 — Classification became a value scale.** The simple-to-complex axis became primitive-to-highly-evolved ranking. Experimental comparison could not resist Western values that claimed a direct line from a universal Greece defined exclusively by Reason, Science, and the “Greek Miracle.” (Detienne, pp. 7–8; PDF pp. 22–23.)

- **C32 — Other societies became precursors or foils.** Once Greece was made the unique source of Western universality, other ancient societies were marginalized and admitted only insofar as they anticipated or contrasted with Greece. (Detienne, pp. 7–8; PDF pp. 22–23.)

- **C33 — Academic Hellenists became guardians of an embalmed patrimony.** The Greco-Roman world, with Athens as capital, was placed off limits to reciprocal comparison; comparison could move only one way. Greece was enclosed in a “vacuum bell jar,” made Patrimony of Humanity, and assigned reliable academic guardians. Detienne calls the result “mummification.” (Detienne, p. 8; PDF p. 23.)

- **C34 — The comparativist is structurally a trespasser.** Detienne adopts Dumézil’s image of a vagabond camping on the properties of princes and lords. Comparison crosses the proprietary borders by which specialists treat objects and territories as possessions. (Detienne, p. 8; PDF p. 23.)

- **C35 — The closure of Greece occurred precisely when ancient societies became newly valuable for cross-cultural thought.** Anthropologists were learning to observe how old and new bodies of knowledge and thought recombined and might be shared across cultures. At that moment Greece was barred from comparison with Iroquois, Polynesians, and peoples designated “savage” or “primitive.” (Detienne, p. 8; PDF p. 23.)

### C. National historical science and the manufacture of peoples outside history

- **C36 — Germany and France jointly forged national historical science.** From the Franco-German conflicts of the nineteenth century through the aftermath of World War I, they made history a science of a past “in itself” whose primary form was national. Detienne contrasts this with British ethnographers traveling through Australia, New Zealand, Polynesia, India, and Borneo, where observation and comparison remained joined. (Detienne, pp. 8–9; PDF pp. 23–24.)

- **C37 — Two models of Nation must be distinguished.** One revolutionary and ecstatic nation uproots itself, denying exclusive attachment to one tradition or ancestral land. The other, associated with Herder, accumulates “useful prejudices,” venerates an ancient language, claims a particular genius, and intoxicates itself with native force, blood, and soil. (Detienne, p. 9; PDF p. 24.)

- **C38 — Herderian nationalism provided the durable model.** Herder’s hostility to Rousseau and Jacobin universalism fed a German objective history led by Ranke; Lavisse and Monod then trained French historical “foot soldiers.” Detienne treats the circulation of this model as transnational even though its object is national uniqueness. (Detienne, p. 9; PDF p. 24.)

- **C39 — Colonial expansion did not create metropolitan French anthropology.** Nineteenth-century France accumulated wealth from colonies in the Caribbean, Ivory Coast, New Caledonia, and Madagascar, yet ethnography was institutionally absent in metropolitan France. Marcel Mauss noted in 1913 the absence of professors and research institutes in ethnography and anthropology. (Detienne, p. 9; PDF p. 24.)

- **C40 — The first French university department that trained anthropologists appeared only in 1986 at Paris-Nanterre.** By then history had long saturated the institutional ladder from universities to village schools. Detienne uses the chronological asymmetry to explain the unequal power of the two disciplines. (Detienne, pp. 9–10; PDF pp. 24–25.)

- **C41 — Academic qualifications made historians a corporation with public authority.** History maintained hegemony in the humanities and social sciences through corporate discipline among historians with credentials at every level. The profession was entrusted with the national conscience and with deciding how France’s history should be written. (Detienne, p. 10; PDF p. 25.)

- **C42 — National historical authority persists into contemporary politics.** Detienne links the 1998 report that a third of France was prepared to follow nationalists to a lineage from Barrès through Vichy to Le Pen and the National Front. This is a polemical continuity claim, not a detailed causal demonstration in this chapter. (Detienne, p. 10; PDF p. 25.)

- **C43 — Barrès’s Dreyfus-era appeal to comparative ethnology shows that comparison itself can racialize.** Barrès declared French and Jewish morality species-specific and suggested Dreyfus be treated as ethnological testimony instead of as a French peer. Detienne calls the appeal paradoxical: the nationalist desired a chair in comparative ethnology in order to authorize exclusion. (Detienne, p. 10; PDF p. 25.)

- **C44 — A para-university chair existed, but its title installed hierarchy.** The Hautes Études established a chair in 1888 for “the religions of noncivilized peoples.” Marcel Mauss and Maurice Leenhardt taught under that title in religious studies. (Detienne, pp. 10–11; PDF pp. 25–26.)

- **C45 — Lévi-Strauss’s 1951 relabeling changed the lack without abolishing it.** As Mauss’s successor, Lévi-Strauss replaced “without civilization” with “without writing,” justified by constraints on oral tradition. Detienne argues that the new designation still organized communities through a presumed deficiency. (Detienne, p. 11; PDF p. 26.)

- **C46 — “Without civilization,” “without writing,” and “without history” are cumulative exclusions.** Communities encountered since the Renaissance were assigned the third lack by the late eighteenth century. The American and French Revolutions’ claims to inaugurate a new historical humanity helped convert absence of the national-historical form from a possible advantage into a defect. (Detienne, p. 11; PDF p. 26.)

- **C47 — Written-document history made exclusion methodologically self-reinforcing.** Once the Nation became civilization’s crowning achievement, historians whose evidence and long tradition centered on writing had little reason to study communities already assigned to natural history. The archive criterion and the civilizational classification validated one another. (Detienne, p. 11; PDF p. 26.)

- **C48 — “Cold” versus “hot” societies repeated the deprivation in a new vocabulary.** Peoples first disinherited, then declared without writing and banished from history, were finally grouped as cold societies against historical hot societies. Detienne says both historicity and France’s fragile anthropology lost through this classification. (Detienne, p. 11; PDF p. 26.)

- **C49 — Greek ancestral status legitimized French culture.** Historical science asserted that Greeks could not be compared with Iroquois or Polynesians because Greeks possessed writing, invented civilization, and first wrote “real history.” Noble Hellenes replaced Trojan ancestors as the source of universal reason and letters that authorized national culture. (Detienne, p. 12; PDF p. 27.)

- **C50 — The Academy enforces the Greek boundary.** “Doing anthropology with the Greeks” is treated as insanity to be disposed of institutionally. Detienne restates his governing historical contrast: historical science was born nationalist, while anthropology was born comparative. (Detienne, p. 12; PDF p. 27.)

### D. Bloch, Britain, Germany, and the institutional containment of comparison

- **C51 — Marc Bloch’s comparativism remained intrafamilial.** His 1928 Oslo plea for a comparative history of European societies asked historians to stop merely placing one national history after another. Yet it remained a project among medieval historians rather than a full exchange between history and anthropology. (Detienne, p. 12; PDF p. 27.)

- **C52 — Canonization can conceal institutional defeat.** Detienne calls him “Saint Marc Bloch” because later hagiography across left and right can obscure how the project was wrecked by “National Antiquities.” Reverence for a past innovator does not establish that the institution accepted the innovation. (Detienne, pp. 12–13; PDF pp. 27–28.)

- **C53 — Bloch’s 1928 and 1933 Collège de France applications reveal the operative boundary.** Both proposed “A comparative history of European societies,” and Bloch refused to replace “European” with “medieval.” The Collège position carried the authority to speak for national consciousness, so a Europe-centered formulation threatened the expected national remit. (Detienne, p. 13; PDF p. 28.)

- **C54 — Febvre advised Bloch to translate comparison into national antiquity.** By presenting French peasantry and rural life as the “real national antiquity,” Bloch could have become a “great historian of France.” Febvre’s advice reveals how professional survival required comparative research to be rhetorically nationalized. (Detienne, p. 13; PDF p. 28.)

- **C55 — “Whatever is national is incomparable” is the institutional axiom.** The Middle Ages, rural life, customs, ancestors, and Gallic, Trojan, Greek, or Homeric roots could all be enclosed within national genius. The problem with Bloch’s title was therefore political and professional, not an absence of comparative substance in rural history. (Detienne, p. 13; PDF p. 28.)

- **C56 — Britain reproduces a moderated version of the same structure.** John H. Elliott’s May 1991 Oxford inaugural lecture, “National and Comparative History,” followed Britain’s prospective European integration and the Berlin Wall’s collapse. He presented comparison as necessary while reassuring the Vice-Chancellor that national history and British identity would remain primary. (Detienne, p. 14; PDF p. 29.)

- **C57 — Elliott’s proposed comparisons remained within great European nations.** British and Spanish national character, or British, Spanish, and French colonization, could be cautiously compared. Braudel’s “France, your name is diversity” supported the claim that diversity need not dissolve a great national identity. (Detienne, p. 14; PDF p. 29.)

- **C58 — The Bloch-Elliott paradigm preserved immediate comparability.** It allowed neighboring, contemporary, similar societies and two or three great nations. Detienne says Elliott never risked any *hybris* by including non-European societies in questions about founding colonies, making territory, or establishing collective life in a new place. (Detienne, pp. 14–15; PDF pp. 29–30.)

- **C59 — Oxford “comparative history” could remain social history inside the professional club.** Historians compared with historians from the same College-world; interdisciplinarity and European expansion did not necessarily unsettle the national or continental boundary. Detienne asks how long the post-Berlin-Wall opening would last and why. (Detienne, p. 15; PDF p. 30.)

- **C60 — Annales illustrates the gap between good intentions and realized comparison.** Detienne says Febvre’s comparative approach remained mostly implicit. Febvre’s recovered *Honneur et patrie*, described by Braudel as a history of the birth of the idea of a motherland, returns even this reformer to the French national object. (Detienne, p. 15; PDF p. 30.)

- **C61 — Detienne admits the account’s polemical and geographically partial character.** Before crossing to Germany, he calls the French presentation exaggerated, under-informed, polemical, and stubbornly Franco-French. This is an explicit warning about argumentative register and coverage. (Detienne, p. 15; PDF p. 30.)

- **C62 — Germany had comparative thinkers without a comparative historical mainstream.** Max Weber, Otto Hintze, Georg Simmel, and Aby Warburg existed, and Franco-German intermediaries later included the Marc Bloch House, Strasbourg commemorations, and bilingual intellectual biographies. Yet since the 1870–71 Reich, dominant history was neither social, European, sociological, nor comparative. (Detienne, pp. 15–16; PDF pp. 30–31.)

- **C63 — Georg von Below and Friedrich Meinecke policed the German profession.** Detienne pairs Below with Ernest Lavisse and Meinecke with Sorbonne gatekeeper Charles Seignobos. Medieval archives, documents, and origins dominated their historical object. (Detienne, p. 16; PDF p. 31.)

- **C64 — Below made nationalist State-centered political history the royal road.** Detienne links it to anti-Semitism and anti-Republicanism and says social history, sociology, and epistemological reflection were denounced. Disciplinary boundary defense thus excluded rival objects and rival modes of reflexivity. (Detienne, p. 16; PDF p. 31.)

- **C65 — Meinecke located “perfect history” between Goethe and Ranke.** Its achievement was supposedly the development of the individual and the State, state policy, and reasons of state in Germany. Detienne’s repeated “State” exposes the national object hidden in a universal claim about historical perfection. (Detienne, p. 16; PDF p. 31.)

- **C66 — German exceptionalism became a shared continental highway.** The *Sonderweg* began as a byway and became, in Detienne’s metaphor, a six-lane motorway on both sides of a median strip. National security, not German uniqueness alone, organized comparable disciplinary closure in France and Germany. Warburg’s transfer of his library to Britain is invoked as recognition of that danger. (Detienne, p. 16; PDF p. 31.)

- **C67 — This past remained active in 1998–99.** Detienne says certain German historians showed that the nationalist past had not passed. The assigned chapter states the claim but relies on its endnote for cases and documentation. (Detienne, p. 16; PDF p. 31.)

### E. Credentialism, peer control, philosophy, and the counterexample of collaboration

- **C68 — The 1988 Annales “crisis in history” could have opened schism and new projects.** Detienne welcomes threats to grand models, yet describes professional defenders who responded by protecting History’s terrain, methods, archives, and records and by rereading Febvre and Bloch to found a new “scientificity of history.” (Detienne, pp. 16–17; PDF pp. 31–32.)

- **C69 — Febvre’s state thesis functions as an icon of credentialed historical seriousness.** *Philippe II et la Franche-Comté* is 780 pages with forty pages devoted solely to sources, an impeccable chronology, and gilt-leather monumental form. Detienne satirizes its elevation into the Hall of Mirrors of great historians who served national reconciliation and patriotic mobilization. (Detienne, p. 17; PDF p. 32.)

- **C70 — The rhetoric of a “more just and egalitarian” profession can intensify gatekeeping.** Detienne attributes to Noiriel a prescription of return to sources, disciplinary reinforcement, more effective control over research permits, and democratically presented peer judgment to decide the problems “common to all historians.” He then exposes “all” as historians of France. (Detienne, p. 17; PDF p. 32.)

- **C71 — Peer rule is shown as both democratic vocabulary and corporate jurisdiction.** The profession defines the legitimate problems, grants permission to research, and treats national salvation as the first priority. The boundary is reproduced through procedural legitimacy rather than only through an explicit nationalist doctrine. (Detienne, p. 17; PDF p. 32.)

- **C72 — Anthropology disappears from the proposed rescue of history.** Ethnology enters only as the discredited “structuralist fad” affecting Bloch’s readers. Marcel Mauss cannot be made a heroic field ancestor because he did not leave the Latin Quarter. (Detienne, pp. 17–18; PDF pp. 32–33.)

- **C73 — Fieldwork becomes anthropology’s own credential and property regime.** Honor goes to ethnologists with thirty years in the field who avoid extrapolation or trespass on a colleague’s neighboring patch. Detienne treats long tenure, non-extrapolation, and territorial ownership as the ethnographic analogue of historical closure. (Detienne, p. 18; PDF p. 33.)

- **C74 — The disciplines occupy unequal credential ranks.** Ethnology lacks the prestige and credentials of history, though it may occasionally be useful. History’s defining concerns become profession, discipline, and strict control of credentials. Detienne’s image of Mauss and Bloch looking at one another across the Sorbonne courtyard captures proximity without intellectual meeting. (Detienne, p. 18; PDF p. 33.)

- **C75 — French philosophy is another closed credential community.** Competitive selection begins in primary school; successful candidates are enclosed in dialogue with Plato and at most two or three “great texts.” The best students may criticize those texts, but only secretly and against institutional expectation. (Detienne, p. 18; PDF p. 33.)

- **C76 — Canonical exhaustiveness prevents curiosity about elsewhere.** The Greeks are made a complete model whose supposedly exhaustive subjects block ideas capable of circumventing school teaching. Credentialed mastery of a tiny canon operates as protection from external comparison. (Detienne, p. 18; PDF p. 33.)

- **C77 — Husserl-to-Heidegger philosophy claims jurisdiction over anthropology’s legitimacy.** Anthropology is declared lost because it allegedly fails to pose the prior question of Reality emanating from Dasein. Detienne reads this as a definitive disciplinary judgment that ends inquiry rather than engaging anthropological evidence. (Detienne, pp. 18–19; PDF pp. 33–34.)

- **C78 — Detienne ties the philosophical hierarchy to spiritual and racial superiority.** Anthropology’s attention to societies labeled without historicity could be condemned as indifference to the spiritual values said to ground a people’s, and in context an “Aryan race’s,” superiority. He polemically claims that this is the gist of *Being and Time* (1927) before Heidegger’s National-Socialist university address of 27 May 1933, and connects it to Heidegger’s later silence. (Detienne, pp. 18–19; PDF pp. 33–34.)

- **C79 — The EPHE sixth section supplies Detienne’s positive institutional counterexample.** In the 1960s, the social and economic sciences section, under the Braudel of *The Mediterranean* and material civilization and capitalism, spent about twenty years reducing mutual prejudice between historians and ethnologists. Detienne grounds this claim in his own 1963 appointment as Director of Studies to create a Center of Comparative Studies on Ancient Societies. (Detienne, p. 19; PDF p. 34.)

- **C80 — Collaboration begins from common problems rather than one discipline’s service to another.** Philosophers who became ethnologists and historians loosened from corporatism recognized war, hunting, and relations to the earth as shared institutional questions. Historians did not ask only how ethnology could serve medieval or ancient history, and anthropologists valued historical discoveries more than guarding their preserves. (Detienne, p. 19; PDF p. 34.)

- **C81 — Experimental comparison requires reciprocal curiosity.** University barbed wire “seemed” forgotten when fields concentrated on the questions bringing them together instead of differences often sustained by ignorance. Detienne’s word “seemed” leaves the permanence of this opening uncertain. (Detienne, pp. 19–20; PDF pp. 34–35.)

- **C82 — Two earlier generations created collaboration outside harmful academic teaching.** From the 1920s through the 1940s, sociological linguists Antoine Meillet and Émile Benveniste, anthropologists Marcel Mauss and Marcel Granet, and the independent Georges Dumézil developed mutual recognition at a distance from university divisions. (Detienne, p. 20; PDF p. 35.)

- **C83 — Language preserves inherited categories of perception and institution.** Syntax and vocabulary several centuries old carry ways of perceiving, analyzing, and coordinating; tradition persists partly subconsciously in language. Researchers should excavate the collective categories and notions that form legal, political, and social institutions. Benveniste calls this an Indo-European “historical subconscious.” (Detienne, p. 20; PDF p. 35.)

- **C84 — “Mythical thought” held the network together.** Mauss, Granet, Dumézil, and their heirs treated mythology from India through China to Hesiodic Greece as thought containing fundamental categories. Its unified language encompassed major social and spiritual configurations and promised access to laws of collective mental activity. (Detienne, p. 20; PDF p. 35.)

- **C85 — Myth was also read as protohistory of rational categories.** As mythology faded or unraveled, it was thought to preserve early conduct and reveal beginnings of legal, political, and religious categories that would later flourish in rational forms. Greek collaboration focused on the advent of law, institution of politics, emergence of ethics, creation of currency, and birth of philosophy. (Detienne, pp. 20–21; PDF pp. 35–36.)

- **C86 — The 1960s brought historians and ethnologists into a shared study of ordinary life and change.** Historians treated eating, believing, and loving in past communities as evidence for understanding human societies; ethnologists studied change, regimes of historicity, and radical transformations of cultures long marked as archaic. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **C87 — Mores and customs reunite the disciplines.** Historians rediscovered a domain that broad inquirers had long explored. Kinship is Detienne’s concrete example: blood links participate in founding social relations and establishing power relations. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **C88 — The final method is to “plug into one particular cultural network.”** Detienne situates local immersion within an admitted-to-be-unbuilt “single great investigation into human societies.” A case is entered deeply, yet it is never treated as a self-sealed world. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **C89 — Proximity and distance are simultaneous requirements.** Historians studying other times and ethnologists studying other places face the same interpretive task: become familiar enough to think as though they belong, while acting as though they do not. Interpretation arises from that maintained double position. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **C90 — Comparative interpretation depends on shared humanity without collapsing variety.** Researchers belong to the same species as the communities they study, but learning concerns the plurality of communities that species forms. Human commonality authorizes comparison; cultural variety supplies its object. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

## III. Consolidated institutional, disciplinary, credentialism, and boundary notes

- **I01 — Boundary maxim:** “Only compare what is comparable” is an authorization rule that makes disciplinary common sense decide admissibility before research begins. It hides boundary-making inside methodological caution. (Detienne, pp. ix–x; PDF pp. 6–7.)

- **I02 — Object ownership:** Historians, Hellenists, and ethnographers treat nations, Greece, field sites, or neighboring patches as professional property. Detienne repeatedly figures constructive comparison as crossing customs posts, barbed wire, preserves, and estates. (Detienne, pp. xii–xiii, 8, 18–20; PDF pp. 9–10, 23, 33–35.)

- **I03 — Career incentives:** Detienne tentatively links retreat from comparison to positions, honors, recognition, decorations, academies, and ennoblement. Comparative closure can therefore be materially and symbolically rewarded without an explicit ban. (Detienne, pp. xii–xiii; PDF pp. 9–10.)

- **I04 — Chair politics:** The 1992 comparative-anthropology-of-Greece chair passed by two votes; Bloch failed in 1928 and 1933 with a comparative-European title; Elliott used an Oxford chair to introduce carefully bounded comparison. Chairs act as sites where legitimate scale and object are negotiated. (Detienne, pp. xii–xiii, 12–15; PDF pp. 9–10, 27–30.)

- **I05 — Credential asymmetry:** France institutionalized historical training from elite universities to village schools long before it created a university anthropology department in 1986. The more credentialed discipline acquired jurisdiction over national conscience. (Detienne, pp. 9–10, 18; PDF pp. 24–25, 33.)

- **I06 — Permit and peer control:** Noiriel’s proposed discipline reinforcement, research-permit control, and peer definition of common problems is Detienne’s clearest example of gatekeeping expressed as democratic and egalitarian governance. (Detienne, p. 17; PDF p. 32.)

- **I07 — Monumental credential forms:** Febvre’s 780-page state thesis, impeccable chronology, forty source pages, and gilt binding exemplify how scale, source apparatus, and format can signify disciplinary seriousness and canonizable authority. Detienne’s satire targets the institutional use of these signs, not source work as such. (Detienne, p. 17; PDF p. 32.)

- **I08 — Credentialism can be anti-intellectual even when it protects rigor.** The defended objects are profession, terrain, methods, archives, records, source return, and scientificity; the excluded possibility is a cross-disciplinary reconstruction of the object and question. (Detienne, pp. 16–18; PDF pp. 31–33.)

- **I09 — Anthropology develops its own credentials:** thirty years of fieldwork, refusal to extrapolate, and respect for a colleague’s patch become its honors. Detienne therefore does not exempt anthropology from professional territoriality. (Detienne, p. 18; PDF p. 33.)

- **I10 — Canonical education is a boundary technology.** Competitive selection and intensive dialogue with Plato and a handful of great texts produce expertise while blocking curiosity about alternative intellectual materials. The issue is the canon’s claim to exhaustiveness. (Detienne, p. 18; PDF p. 33.)

- **I11 — Labels of lack police disciplinary jurisdiction.** “Without civilization,” “without writing,” “without history,” and “cold” make peoples unsuitable for historians whose recognized evidence is writing and whose object is national historical development. (Detienne, pp. 10–12; PDF pp. 25–27.)

- **I12 — Universal patrimony can conceal custodial monopoly.** Making Greece the Patrimony of Humanity sounds universal, yet it licenses handpicked Hellenist guardians to block reciprocal comparison. Universal ownership and specialist enclosure can operate together. (Detienne, p. 8; PDF p. 23.)

- **I13 — Disciplines are shaped by states and nations.** Detienne links French and German historical science to war, national consciousness, schooling, public identity, anti-Republicanism, anti-Semitism, and state-centered political history. Disciplinary boundaries are therefore political institutions as well as divisions of cognitive labor. (Detienne, pp. 8–10, 16; PDF pp. 23–25, 31.)

- **I14 — Comparison may be allowed when it leaves the unit of prestige intact.** Elliott can compare Britain with Spain and France while keeping national identity primary; local historical anthropologies can borrow anthropological language while remaining entirely inside Greece; social history can be called comparative when practiced within a professional club. (Detienne, pp. xii, 14–15; PDF pp. 9, 29–30.)

- **I15 — Nationalism travels beneath assertions of uniqueness.** Ranke’s German model trains French historians; Bloch and Elliott converge on similar-society comparison; French and German professions share state-centered closure. The institutional form is transnational even as each national object is declared incomparable. (Detienne, pp. 9, 13–16; PDF pp. 24, 28–31.)

- **I16 — Collaboration requires reciprocal use rather than disciplinary service.** Detienne praises historians interested in what ethnologists know and anthropologists interested in historical societies, without either treating the other merely as an auxiliary or protecting a sealed domain. (Detienne, p. 19; PDF p. 34.)

- **I17 — Shared questions can replace shared credentials as the organizing bond.** War, hunting, land, language, myth, kinship, law, politics, ethics, currency, and philosophy become joint research objects because each discipline possesses partial access to the problem. (Detienne, pp. 19–21; PDF pp. 34–36.)

- **I18 — Constructive comparison needs local competence and cross-case freedom.** Intracultural analysis and deep insertion into a cultural network precede comparison, while the analyst retains distance and permission to reassemble relations across chronology and territory. Expertise is retained but ceases to confer exclusive ownership. (Detienne, pp. x, xiv–xv, 21; PDF pp. 7, 11–12, 36.)

- **I19 — Crisis can harden boundaries.** The Annales crisis could enable new projects, yet professional insecurity mobilized defense of terrain, masters, and methods. Threat to a paradigm can therefore produce renewed credential policing rather than inquiry. (Detienne, pp. 16–17; PDF pp. 31–32.)

- **I20 — Counter-institutions matter.** The Hautes Études chair, EPHE sixth section, Detienne’s center, the Marc Bloch House, and research networks outside standard academic teaching show that institutional arrangements can either impose lack and hierarchy or make cross-disciplinary work possible. (Detienne, pp. 10, 15, 19–20; PDF pp. 25, 30, 34–35.)

## IV. Objections, tensions, and limits internal to the assigned text

The following are analytical observations about the argument as presented in these pages. They are not attributed to Detienne as settled conclusions unless the item records his explicit caveat.

- **T01 — Explicit genealogical caveat.** Detienne admits that his Renaissance “beginning” may not satisfy strict genealogy. The scene functions as a productive ancestry for constructive comparativism, so it cannot simultaneously carry the full evidentiary burden of proving a continuous historical lineage. (Detienne, p. 4; PDF p. 19.)

- **T02 — Explicit polemical caveat.** Detienne calls his own French presentation exaggerated, under-informed, polemical, and Franco-French. Its satirical portraits should be read as a manifesto’s institutional diagnosis, with the endnotes and later chapters needed for full historical adjudication. (Detienne, p. 15; PDF p. 30.)

- **T03 — The history/anthropology opposition is deliberately sharp but not stable across his evidence.** He says historical science was born nationalist and anthropology naturally comparative, yet he also documents Bloch, Febvre, Braudel, and collaborative historians, along with territorial ethnologists and hierarchical anthropology. The genealogy identifies dominant institutional tendencies rather than clean disciplinary essences. (Detienne, pp. x–xii, 7–8, 12–21; PDF pp. 7–9, 22–23, 27–36.)

- **T04 — Comparison is not inherently emancipatory.** Lafitau’s Christian degeneration model, Fontenelle’s infantilization, evolutionist ranking, Barrès’s racial comparison, and Castilian physical experiments all show that expanding the comparative field can reproduce theology, racism, colonial violence, and civilizational hierarchy. Constructing comparables requires normative and evidentiary controls that are not yet specified here. (Detienne, pp. 4–11; PDF pp. 19–26.)

- **T05 — Rejecting typology sits in tension with reducing variables.** Detienne says constructive comparison is not meant to establish typologies or morphologies, yet he also proposes reducing variables and focusing on those “of a similar nature.” The assigned pages do not define the point at which a useful variable set solidifies into the kind of type he rejects. (Detienne, pp. xi, xiv; PDF pp. 8, 11.)

- **T06 — Constructed comparability needs selection criteria.** The text explains that objects, gestures, situations, relations, and positions may be selected as operators or litmus papers, but it does not yet specify how to justify a selection, test a failed comparison, weigh disanalogies, or prevent a researcher from manufacturing the desired reaction. (Detienne, pp. ix, xiv–xv; PDF pp. 6, 11–12.)

- **T07 — Temporary bracketing risks permanent flattening.** In polytheistic analysis, individual divine traits are set aside with a promise to return later. The procedure can disclose relational properties, but the assigned pages do not provide a safeguard ensuring that context, history, or specificity is actually restored after manipulation. (Detienne, p. xiv; PDF p. 11.)

- **T08 — Freedom from chronology is productive and dangerous.** “Upsetting history” and chronology allows latent structural relations to emerge, yet it can also erase sequence, transmission, causality, and transformation. The text celebrates the freedom without giving a rule for when diachronic order must constrain the comparison. (Detienne, pp. xiv–xv; PDF pp. 11–12.)

- **T09 — Greek material remains unusually central even in the critique of Greek centrality.** Detienne converts Greece from norm into “operator,” which is a substantive methodological change. Still, the archive and examples repeatedly return to Greece, so a reader may ask whether operational centrality can continue to privilege the same canon under a new rationale. (Detienne, pp. xi–xii, 5–8, 20–21; PDF pp. 8–9, 20–23, 35–36.)

- **T10 — The myth-to-reason narrative risks reinstating the evolutionism Detienne criticizes.** On pp. 7–8 he condemns the path from primitive to evolved and Greece as the source of Reason. On pp. 20–21 he reports approvingly that mythology fades while legal, political, religious, and rational forms flourish, especially in Greece. The latter research program may preserve a developmental hierarchy unless “emergence” is treated as plural and non-ranked. (Detienne, pp. 7–8, 20–21; PDF pp. 22–23, 35–36.)

- **T11 — Nationalist origins do not by themselves settle later validity.** The genealogy powerfully exposes objects and exclusions built into historical science, but a discipline’s origin cannot alone prove that each later method or conclusion remains nationalist. Detienne’s own positive historian collaborators show the need to distinguish inherited structure from transformed practice. (Detienne, pp. 8–21; PDF pp. 23–36.)

- **T12 — Institutional incentives are sometimes posed rather than demonstrated.** Detienne asks whether Finley and Vernant retreated for cultural-specificity reasons or in pursuit of advantages and honors. The question identifies plausible structural pressures while stopping short of evidence about their motives. (Detienne, p. xii; PDF p. 9.)

- **T13 — The Heidegger judgment is stronger than the argument supplied in this chapter.** Detienne asserts that racial-spiritual superiority is the gist of *Being and Time* and invokes the chronology from 1927 to 1933 and Heidegger’s silence. These pages do not perform the textual demonstration needed to distinguish influence, implication, and explicit thesis. (Detienne, pp. 18–19; PDF pp. 33–34.)

- **T14 — Colonial conditions remain underanalyzed.** The text notes colonial wealth, British travel, Castilian classification, and racialized comparison. It does not systematically analyze how conquest, collection, mobility, translation, and unequal access produced the comparative archives it celebrates. (Detienne, pp. 1–10; PDF pp. 16–25.)

- **T15 — “All that is needed” understates the institutional problem Detienne has established.** His conclusion says prejudices need to be wiped out, but earlier pages show chairs, curricula, credentials, permits, archives, national authority, and career rewards reproducing the division. The positive EPHE episode suggests institutional redesign, not attitude change alone, is necessary. (Detienne, pp. 17–21; PDF pp. 32–36.)

- **T16 — The final “single great investigation” is explicitly not yet built.** Detienne admits that its context remains to be established. The chapter therefore ends with an orientation—deep insertion plus distance, common humanity plus variety—rather than an operational protocol or governance structure. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **T17 — Proximity and distance name a real interpretive tension without resolving it.** Thinking as though one belongs can reduce external caricature, while acting as though one does not can preserve analysis. The chapter does not specify how researchers recognize over-identification, alien projection, or disagreements between insiders and comparative interpreters. (Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **T18 — Breadth can become totalization.** The ambitions to represent everything, investigate human societies as a whole, and recover laws of collective mental activity are generative. They also risk replacing national universalism with a comparativist universalism unless plurality, source limits, and interpretive contestation remain structurally protected. (Detienne, pp. 3, 20–21; PDF pp. 18, 35–36.)

## V. Provisional Polymyth relevance — analytical inferences only, not project rulings

- **P01 — [Polymyth inference] Comparability should be an output of a method.** A Polymyth comparison could record which relation, function, gesture, narrative operation, or institutional position makes two unlike materials comparable, instead of assuming that shared genre, name, period, or motif is sufficient. This follows Detienne’s move from immediately similar objects to constructed comparables. (Basis: Detienne, pp. ix–xi; PDF pp. 6–8.)

- **P02 — [Polymyth inference] Intracultural analysis should precede cross-cultural synthesis.** Tylor’s comparison of already-analyzed sets of relations implies that a work, mythic system, or cultural network should first be described on its own terms before its relations are compared. (Basis: Detienne, p. x; PDF p. 7.)

- **P03 — [Polymyth inference] “Similar stories” is weaker than “comparable configurations.”** Lafitau’s comparison of sacrifice, abstinence, initiation, and duties suggests tracking relational constellations rather than isolated motifs, character names, or plot resemblances. (Basis: Detienne, p. 6; PDF p. 21.)

- **P04 — [Polymyth inference] The project can use deliberately distant cases as tests.** Ethiopia, Cossack foundations, Italian communes, Vedic historicity, Roman pontiffs, and Amerindian territorial practice are valuable because they break the apparent naturalness of the Athens-to-1789 road. Distant cases can reveal a variable that a familiar lineage hides. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xiii–xiv; PDF pp. 10–11.)

- **P05 — [Polymyth inference] Mythic figures may be analyzed positionally before being treated as personalities.** Detienne’s polytheistic procedure suggests asking what positions, combinations, objects, gestures, and situations activate a power. This could support a relational comparison of figures while preserving a later return to their individual histories. (Basis: Detienne, p. xiv; PDF p. 11.)

- **P06 — [Polymyth inference] Every analytical bracketing should include a return-to-specificity obligation.** Detienne says individual divine characteristics may be set aside only provisionally. A robust workflow could therefore require a second pass that restores cultural, narrative, historical, and linguistic specificity after relational testing. (Basis: Detienne, p. xiv; PDF p. 11.)

- **P07 — [Polymyth inference] Chronology and genealogy should be separate comparison dimensions, not automatic vetoes.** Detienne permits rearrangement across periods to expose intellectual operations, while the internal tension identified above requires chronology to return when transmission, causation, or transformation is claimed. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xiv–xv; PDF pp. 11–12.)

- **P08 — [Polymyth inference] Canonical status is not evidence of greater coherence or salience.** The production of Greece as “incomparable,” universal, and guarded shows how institutions can mistake inherited prestige for an analytical result. A Polymyth ranking would need criteria independent of patrimony and academic custody. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xi, 7–8; PDF pp. 8, 22–23.)

- **P09 — [Polymyth inference] A tradition can function as an operator without functioning as the norm.** Detienne’s “Greeks as excellent operators” offers a way to use a dense, well-studied archive to generate questions across cases while refusing to make it the developmental endpoint or standard of value. (Basis: Detienne, p. xii; PDF p. 9.)

- **P10 — [Polymyth inference] Polymyth work should distinguish analytical scale from inherited disciplinary scale.** A nation, civilization, religion, character, motif, corpus, or historical period may be too large or too proprietorial to function as the actual comparable. The operative unit may instead be a set of relations or a practice configuration. (Basis: Detienne, pp. ix–xi, 5–8; PDF pp. 6–8, 20–23.)

- **P11 — [Polymyth inference] Boundary statements need an authorization audit.** Claims such as “these works cannot be compared,” “this belongs only to one discipline,” or “this corpus is unique” can be tested for who gains custody, what variables are suppressed, and which comparisons become impossible. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xii–xiii, 8, 12–18; PDF pp. 9–10, 23, 27–33.)

- **P12 — [Polymyth inference] Credentialism critique should target jurisdiction, not evidence or expertise in themselves.** Detienne values local immersion, philology, field knowledge, documents, and sources, while criticizing their conversion into exclusive terrain, permit control, canonized format, and professional monopoly. The distinction prevents an anti-credential analysis from collapsing into anti-scholarship. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 2, 9–10, 17–21; PDF pp. 17, 24–25, 32–36.)

- **P13 — [Polymyth inference] Comparison needs an explicit anti-hierarchy check.** Lafitau, Fontenelle, evolutionism, and Barrès demonstrate that a formally broad comparison can still be Christian, civilizational, racial, or national in its explanatory standard. A comparison should therefore name whether one case is silently functioning as origin, norm, adult, rational endpoint, or judge. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 6–11; PDF pp. 21–26.)

- **P14 — [Polymyth inference] “Without” categories can create false absences.** Classifications such as without writing, history, civilization, or historical heat show how a framework can define its own preferred evidence and then treat other forms as missing. Polymyth categories could be checked for whether they recognize alternate realizations of memory, historicity, narrative, or thought. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 10–12; PDF pp. 25–27.)

- **P15 — [Polymyth inference] Institutional histories belong inside the interpretation of canon formation.** The Greek Miracle, national historical science, chairs, school systems, professional corporations, and academic guardians show that corpus prominence and conceptual centrality have institutional causes. This does not settle a work’s meaning, but it is relevant to why some materials become the comparison center. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 7–18; PDF pp. 22–33.)

- **P16 — [Polymyth inference] Shared questions can organize collaboration across distinct expertise.** War, hunting, earth, kinship, language, myth, law, politics, ethics, money, and philosophy operate as problem-spaces in which no single discipline owns the whole object. A Polymyth research structure could preserve area expertise while grouping work around such questions. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 19–21; PDF pp. 34–36.)

- **P17 — [Polymyth inference] A source should be treated simultaneously as a cultural network and as a participant in wider configurations.** Detienne’s final proximity-distance method supports full local reading followed by controlled comparison, rather than either isolated textualism or decontextualized motif collection. (Basis: Detienne, p. 21; PDF p. 36.)

- **P18 — [Polymyth inference] Comparative failures should remain data.** Because comparables are experimentally constructed, a selected object, gesture, or situation that produces no illuminating reaction could show that the proposed variable is wrong or too coarse. Detienne does not formalize this, but it follows from treating comparison as experiment rather than confirmation. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xiv–xv; PDF pp. 11–12.)

- **P19 — [Polymyth inference] A comparison record should separate resemblance, relation, genealogy, value, and function.** Detienne’s critique repeatedly shows category drift: resemblance becomes value, evolutionary order becomes superiority, patrimony becomes monopoly, and writing becomes history. Keeping these claim types distinct would reduce silent promotion from observation to hierarchy. (Basis: Detienne, pp. ix, 6–12; PDF pp. 6, 21–27.)

- **P20 — [Polymyth inference] Multiple beginnings should replace a single canonical road where the evidence supports them.** Ethiopian democracy, Cossack and communal assemblies, plural historicities, and multiple territorial practices model a search for distinct inventions and beginnings without forcing them into one diffusion line. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xiii–xiv; PDF pp. 10–11.)

- **P21 — [Polymyth inference] Myth-to-reason narratives require special scrutiny.** Detienne’s own later formulation partly reproduces the developmental structure he criticizes. Polymyth analysis could treat rational, legal, political, and mythic forms as recombinations and coexistences unless a source establishes a one-way succession. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 7–8, 20–21; PDF pp. 22–23, 35–36.)

- **P22 — [Polymyth inference] Criticism from inside a tradition is an important comparison event.** La Popelinière’s Huguenot history was condemned by his own jurisdiction because distance allowed him to specify different forms of belief. Internal divergence may therefore be analytically more revealing than a simple tradition-versus-outsider contrast. (Basis: Detienne, p. 4; PDF p. 19.)

- **P23 — [Polymyth inference] Comparative research should retain provenance for categories.** “Religion,” “mythology,” “civil religion,” “primitive,” “reason,” “history,” and “culture” enter the archive through specific theological, evolutionary, national, and disciplinary projects. Recording category lineage can prevent a borrowed term from silently carrying its old hierarchy. (Basis: Detienne, pp. 5–12; PDF pp. 20–27.)

- **P24 — [Polymyth inference] Cross-disciplinary synthesis needs reciprocal revision rights.** Detienne’s positive EPHE case works because historians and ethnologists both learn from the other’s material. A synthesis dominated by one discipline’s questions while using others as evidence would reproduce the service relation he rejects. (Basis: Detienne, p. 19; PDF p. 34.)

- **P25 — [Polymyth inference] The method’s final standard is interpretive gain, not mere breadth.** The point of distant cases and experimental manipulation is to reveal an unnoticed aspect, unexpected angle, concealed property, or new variable. Accumulating many traditions without such a reaction would not by itself satisfy Detienne’s constructive comparativism. (Basis: Detienne, pp. xiv–xv; PDF pp. 11–12.)

## Compact statement of the assigned pages’ central movement

Detienne begins by arguing that comparability is constructed through selected relations and experimental questions. He then gives an institutional genealogy of why French and German historical science, nationalist identity, Greek exceptionalism, professional custody, credentials, permits, canons, and disciplinary property suppressed that construction. His counter-tradition runs from Renaissance “perfect history” through early comparative anthropology and selected twentieth-century collaborations. The chapter ends with a double methodological demand: enter a cultural network deeply enough to think from within it, retain enough distance to interpret it, and connect that local competence to a larger investigation of the plurality of human societies. (Detienne, pp. ix–xv, 1–21; PDF pp. 6–12, 16–36.)
