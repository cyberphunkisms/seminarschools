# Marcel Detienne, *Comparing the Incomparable*, Chapter 4: exhaustive research notes

## Scope, status, and citation key

- **Source:** Marcel Detienne, *Comparing the Incomparable*, trans. Janet Lloyd, chapter 4, “Experimenting in the field of polytheisms,” printed pp. 57–78, PDF pp. 72–93.
- **Coverage:** Every page in the assigned chapter was read from the extracted text and checked against rendered images of the source PDF where the OCR was uncertain. The chapter’s endnotes, printed pp. 106–108, PDF pp. 121–123, were also checked for source lineage.
- **Citation format:** “p. 57 / PDF 72” means the printed page number followed by the one-indexed PDF page number.
- **Claim discipline:** Sections headed **Detienne** report or closely paraphrase Detienne’s argument. Sections headed **Provisional Polymyth inference** are applications inferred here and are not Detienne’s claims or ratified project rules.
- **Terminological warning:** This chapter uses “boundary” in at least four separable senses: a deity’s field of action, a material/spatial limit such as a threshold or precinct, an analytic limit placed on a comparison, and a disciplinary/institutional boundary. It does not discuss interpersonal boundary discourse directly.

## Core argument in compressed form — Detienne

1. The appropriate primary unit for the study of polytheism is the **configuration of powers**, rather than the deity treated as a self-contained individual. Polytheism is a complex relational system of associations, oppositions, complementarities, hierarchies, overlaps, and situational groupings. [pp. 59–60 / PDF 74–75]
2. Nineteenth-century comparison missed this object in two ways. It treated recurrent ritual as the stable cross-cultural unit, while treating divine beings as fluid figures that therefore required separate, individual explanation. Its governing questions concerned genesis, evolution, and the origin of divinity. [pp. 57–58 / PDF 72–73]
3. Greek religion was then enclosed as a privileged, culturally self-sufficient domain controlled by specialist historians, while other polytheisms were left to anthropology. The resulting disciplinary partition obstructed comparison across ancient and contemporary polytheistic systems. [pp. 58–59 / PDF 73–74]
4. Georges Dumézil’s structural turn made interactive groups of gods analytically central and redirected attention toward ordered lists, hierarchy, opposition, complementarity, and microsystems. [pp. 59–60 / PDF 74–75]
5. Dumézil’s first durable contribution was recognition of the **already-structural** evidence presented by shared altars, multi-deity sanctuaries, festivals, rituals, recurring pairs, triads, and larger groupings. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
6. His second durable contribution was the principle that no deity can be defined statically. Analysis must map the full range of the deity’s positions, associations, contrasts, limits, and relations within the polytheistic group. [pp. 60–61 / PDF 75–76]
7. His third contribution was the distinction between a deity’s **field of action**, where the deity intervenes, and **mode of action**, how the deity intervenes. This was intended to detect a deity’s constant manner and means across different domains. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
8. That third principle produced a regression. Requiring one precise, god-specific, constant mode of action reinstated the individuated god and the static pantheon that relational analysis had begun to displace. Dumézil’s concentration on major “boss-gods” also marginalized lateral and accessory powers. [pp. 62, 64 / PDF 77, 79]
9. Homeric allocations of divine *timē* and *erga* provide one model of bounded divine provinces, but Greek practice supplies a second model in which every large domain is crossed and shared by multiple powers. Marriage and war are Detienne’s clearest examples. [pp. 62–63 / PDF 77–78]
10. Detienne proposes a microanalytic and experimental procedure that starts with attested groupings, selects concrete objects, gestures, or segments of situations as “litmus papers,” and observes the different reactions produced when powers and configurations are brought into contact. [pp. 65–71 / PDF 80–86]
11. Concrete comparison is discriminating because a narrowly delimited item, such as a horse, bit, foot, stone, road, or threshold, exposes differences obscured by comparisons between whole divine personalities. [pp. 69–73 / PDF 84–88]
12. A concrete object has indefinitely many possible properties and associations, so comparison requires deep knowledge of the culture’s material, ritual, institutional, linguistic, ecological, and technical contexts. Context constrains the otherwise limitless association of features. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
13. The inquiry is recursive. A manipulation may reveal an unsuspected feature of a deity or object, after which other configurations can confirm, correct, or reject the proposed relation. [pp. 66, 71, 77 / PDF 81, 86, 92]
14. “Manipulation” is ultimately more accurate than “experiment,” because historical configurations cannot be repeated under altered conditions. They can nevertheless recur formally across sufficiently varied contexts to expose transformations in their constituent relations. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
15. Structural microanalysis does not erase iconic individuality, personalized narrative, locality, historical change, or deliberate political reorganization. These operate within a system whose combinations are variable yet constrained rather than arbitrary. [pp. 77–78 / PDF 92–93]
16. The culminating claim is that divine powers are connected through micronetworks spanning the social and natural field, and that **every god is plural**. Greek evidence is one small part of a much larger comparative field that requires collaboration between anthropology and history. [p. 78 / PDF 93]

## Page-by-page exhaustive notes — Detienne

### Printed p. 57 / PDF 72

- Early anthropologists already recommended inventorying the different powers active in a village or kingdom; treating gods as research objects is therefore older than contemporary anthropology. [p. 57 / PDF 72]
- Detienne identifies two late nineteenth-century assumptions as obstacles to analysing large groups of supernatural powers in prestigious “historical” civilizations such as China, India, and Greece. [p. 57 / PDF 72]
- From Tylor to Frazer, early comparativists tended to identify ritual organized around one theme, especially calendrical festivals and holy days, as the stable or permanent element across cultures. [p. 57 / PDF 72]
- This assumption directed comparative attention toward seasonal ceremonies and magic kings across Africa, India, Italy, and Scandinavia. [p. 57 / PDF 72]
- At the same time, anthropologists treated divine entities as fluid, inconsistent figures that appeared and disappeared without apparent reason. [p. 57 / PDF 72]
- Once a divine figure possessed a name and individual characteristics, this approach treated it as requiring a separate explanation. The conjunction of stable ritual and unstable gods predisposed scholarship against relational pantheon analysis. [p. 57 / PDF 72]

### Printed p. 58 / PDF 73

- Nineteenth-century ethnology and history of religions were governed by genetic and evolutionary questions. Animism, totemism, soul representations, spirit types, and superior entities were studied as paths toward the origin and development of the idea of the divine. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- Detienne juxtaposes de Brosses’s treatment of the inhabitants of “Nigritia” as witnesses to primordial idolatry and fetishism with the distinct intellectual handling of Greek religion. The juxtaposition exposes a hierarchy between peoples cast as survivals of origins and Greece cast as philosophy’s privileged object. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- Hegel recognized an Olympus populated by coexisting gods with individual lives, passions, interests, and conflicts. The gods ceased to be merely cold allegories and became signifying forms. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- Hegel nevertheless judged the polytheistic world impossible to organize as a systematically articulated whole. Detienne presents this as an early recognition of plurality that still fails to grasp relational system. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- Creuzer and Welcker sought an intuition of the absolute among the Greek gods rather than examining the varying ways Pausanias observed gods grouped together across Greece. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- In the early twentieth century, the division deepened between anthropological “gods of crossroads” and the supposedly paradigmatic great gods of Greece. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- Greek polytheism was enclosed within Greek culture and became the private preserve of its “patented historians,” whom Detienne depicts as resistant to comparison with polytheistic groups past or present. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
- Between the 1920s and 1940s, Greek religion became the site of a polite dispute between positivist historians and spiritual interpreters. Martin P. Nilsson sought simple underlying representations later elaborated by imagination and history. [p. 58 / PDF 73]

### Printed p. 59 / PDF 74

- Walter F. Otto’s intuitive hermeneutics treated each god as the signification of a sphere of existence and an exemplary human experience “present in the world.” [p. 59 / PDF 74]
- Detienne grants merit to both Nilsson and Otto, while arguing that each is indifferent to polytheism’s actual object: a complex relational system among diverse supernatural powers and entities. [p. 59 / PDF 74]
- Dumézil’s influence in the 1960s redirected historians and anthropologists toward polytheistic groups as systems in their own right and toward how Greece, Rome, and India represented themselves through divine entities. [p. 59 / PDF 74]
- Dumézil replaced the dominant historico-genetic approach with structural analysis of complementarities, oppositions, hierarchies, and relational complexes. [p. 59 / PDF 74]
- His work on dispersed configurations and microsystems within the Indo-European domain influenced work on the Caucasus, Greece, and India. [p. 59 / PDF 74]
- The institutional name of Dumézil’s research field changed with its intellectual object. “Comparative mythology,” selected in 1935, was eclipsed in 1945 and gave way by 1948 to “Indo-European civilization” at the Collège de France and in related scholarly circles. [p. 59 / PDF 74]
- Dumézil’s starting object was interactive gods, gods in groups, and collections of powers. Detienne contrasts this with an older comparative mythology that sought divine essences and etymologies through linguistic equations. [p. 59 / PDF 74]

### Printed p. 60 / PDF 75

- The newer Indo-European comparativism studied the order of divine lists, hierarchical relations, opposition, and complementarity. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- Exemplary structured data included the pre-Capitoline triad Jupiter–Mars–Quirinus, the Uppsala triad Odin–Thor–Freya, the Adityas of ancient India, and the Zoroastrian Amesha Spenta of ancient Iran. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- These complex yet primary data established Dumézil’s “fact of structure” and supported an “ultra-history” of Indo-European civilization reconstructed through interconnected concepts and structured common vocabulary. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- Detienne’s mention of proliferating linguistic asterisks and an invariably proto- or prehistoric framework carries a skeptical edge toward ultra-historical reconstruction, even while he preserves its structural insights. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- The first principle transferable beyond the Indo-European field is to attend to immediately visible structures: plural altars, shared sanctuaries, festivals joining two deities, and ritually contrasted aspects of one power. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- Polytheistic groupings can be circumstantial or recurrent, monumental or ephemeral. The analyst’s raw material is thus a field of heterogeneous yet already configured relations. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- Detienne says the structures are already there and warns the analyst against trampling them before interpretation. “Already-structural” evidence precedes the analyst’s own relational hunt. [p. 60 / PDF 75]
- The second transferable principle rejects static definitions of a god and instead requires identification of the entire range of positions occupied within a polytheistic group. [p. 60 / PDF 75]

### Printed p. 61 / PDF 76

- The analyst must supplement what is explicitly or “clearly manifest” with every discoverable form of association and contrast that the culture tries, including implicit ones. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- Studying gods relationally involves defining their respective limits and the boundaries between their fields of action. These boundaries are analytical results of comparison, rather than self-evident isolated properties. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- A major Dumézilian assumption held that the best validation of a proposed relation or divine field would come from a native statement, especially a theological one. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- Polytheistic theologians are described as “administrators of memory” who transmitted both conscious belief and material deposited in the historical subconscious of language and civilization. This makes linguistic and theological testimony simultaneously explicit evidence and a carrier of unacknowledged history. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- Dumézil’s final principle, formulated in 1949 and confirmed in 1966, distinguishes a divine field of action from a mode of action. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- A field of action consists of places and occasions of intervention. A deity’s function may become sufficiently extensive that the deity intervenes far outside what appears to be the original province. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- The proposed question therefore becomes how a god acts rather than where the god appears. Mode of action seeks a constant manner and constant means across scenes. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
- Dumézil required the mode to be precise and specific to one deity. Detienne signals that this innovative requirement conflicts with the tripartite structure underlying Dumézil’s own Indo-European project. [p. 61 / PDF 76]

### Printed p. 62 / PDF 77

- Within Dumézil’s tripartition, function means social activity: wielding power, waging war, or producing goods and food. [p. 62 / PDF 77]
- The most important gods preside, singly or in pairs, over major functions. Dumézil calls these active agents *patrons* or “boss-gods.” [p. 62 / PDF 77]
- Although Dumézil studies relations between figures and different aspects within functions, he tends to foreground the basic characteristics of boss-gods and set aside powers providing lateral or accessory services. [p. 62 / PDF 77]
- The formula “basic characteristics of the boss-gods” resembles a Greek model attested from Homer to Herodotus: every power receives a *timē*, a prerogative or field bounded by the terms of its allotment. [p. 62 / PDF 77]
- Zeus’s post-Titan distribution assigns each deity *erga*, tasks, work, or activities that gods and humans recognize in speech and practice. [p. 62 / PDF 77]
- In *Iliad* V, Diomedes identifies Aphrodite as lacking battle strength and distinguishes her from Athena and Enyo. He wounds her while she protects Aeneas. [p. 62 / PDF 77]
- Zeus then tells Aphrodite that martial *erga* do not belong to her province and directs her toward marriage and love. The episode presents divine functions as explicitly delimited. [pp. 62–63 / PDF 77–78]

### Printed p. 63 / PDF 78

- The apparent separation of war and marriage immediately breaks down under configurational inspection. Aphrodite does not act alone in marriage and love; Hera Teleia, Zeus Teleios, the Charites, Horai, Hermes, and Artemis also operate there. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- War likewise mobilizes Athena, Ares, Enyo, Enyalios, and the Ares–Aphrodite couple stationed at the gates of war in Argos and perhaps Crete. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- The *Iliad* scene excludes one configuration of Aphrodite because the Aphrodite associated with Ares would be inappropriate in that specific confrontation with Diomedes and Athena. A deity’s absence in one scene cannot establish a universal incapacity. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- Detienne names two coexisting Greek models. One assigns bounded domains that gods must not encroach upon and humans must ritually acknowledge. The other shows each large domain crossed or shared by numerous powers. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- Each power handles a particular aspect or dimension whose meaning may be partly concrete and partly abstract. A domain therefore organizes differentiated participation rather than exclusive ownership. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- Detienne argues that Greek polytheism exceeds both Zeus’s simplified Olympian allocation and Dumézil’s bold separation of modes of action. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- Dumézil’s work continues the Maussian search for categories and classifications. Treating the gods as a society made the pantheon a large classification system. [p. 63 / PDF 78]
- Mitra and Varuna within sovereignty supplied a formal classificatory principle, while tripartition exposed structured groups and ways of classifying forces that animate society and the world. [p. 63 / PDF 78]

### Printed p. 64 / PDF 79

- Detienne retains the value of identifying categories and classificatory forms for a historical and variable understanding of the human spirit. [p. 64 / PDF 79]
- He then identifies the central regression in Dumézil’s method. Defining deities by the intentions and means of action can reinforce the traditional individuated god recognizable by constant features. [p. 64 / PDF 79]
- A full positional map had destabilized fixed identity, but a single constant mode reinstated a static pantheon of separately classified agent-gods. [p. 64 / PDF 79]
- The emphasis on a boss-god’s fundamental characteristics thus returns analysis to classical pantheon monographs. [p. 64 / PDF 79]
- Detienne explicitly connects this epistemic regression to academic reproduction. Thesis writers committed to deity monographs can regard the return as vindication, and the form remains career-safe under peer judgment and the confidence of an academic “boss.” [p. 64 / PDF 79]

### Printed p. 65 / PDF 80

- Detienne proposes experimentation first in the Greek polytheisms he knows best. His earlier work compared powers sharing equestrian, navigational, and metallurgical fields but intervening differently. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- He adopts two Dumézilian rules: review the full series of a deity’s positions, and begin with habitual groupings or mythical and religious associations between two or more powers. [pp. 64–65 / PDF 79–80]
- Investigation should move from the most explicit relational contexts toward less obvious and less familiar ones. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- Historians of Greek religion whom Detienne calls institutional guarantors of monographic interpretation reacted critically to these principles. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- Detienne’s structural analyses of *mētis*, cunning intelligence, studied Athena–Poseidon and Athena–Hephaestus to distinguish how associated gods differently oriented a shared practical intelligence. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- The common background was *Mētis* both as a form of intelligence and as a cosmogonic divine power swallowed by the future ruler Zeus. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- Shared skills provided fields for differentiation: metallurgy linked Athena, Hephaestus, and the Telchines; navigation provided another field; management of the horse linked Athena and Poseidon. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- Detienne retrospectively identifies experimental limits in his 1970–74 analysis of Poseidon Hippios and Athena Hippia. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- In the Corinthian configuration, Poseidon governed the horse’s violence, impetuosity, and uncontrolled natural strength, while Athena operated through the metal bit that permits intelligent mastery. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
- The pair appeared complementary: Poseidon was the equine power and Athena the equestrian power. Yet the analysis remained too concentrated on one mode for each deity and on Athena’s *mētis*. [pp. 65–66 / PDF 80–81]

### Printed p. 66 / PDF 81

- Athena can appear as the daughter of Mētis and Zeus or as the gods’ highest embodiment of *mētis*. The earlier analysis used stories and rituals to find configurations resembling the Corinthian pair. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- Those results suggest experiments capable of challenging the requirement of a single constant mode of action. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- The horse can be analysed under both Athena and Poseidon aspects, then brought into relation with Ares, whose martial configuration includes abundant horses and sacrificed horses. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- Hera provides another manipulation. As deity of Argos, desirous of sovereign power, bellicose, and explicitly *hippia*, she can alter the comparison’s understanding of horse, war, sovereignty, and gendered divine roles. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- Arcadian Demeter provides another. The black horse-headed Erinys Demeter and Arion, born from mare-Demeter and stallion-Poseidon, introduce harnessed, mounted, untamed, destructive, and inspired dimensions of the horse. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- Ares beside Athena or Demeter beside Poseidon may disclose unsuspected dimensions of horses and, conversely, new dimensions of Ares, Hera, or Demeter. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- Further manipulations should confirm, correct, or reject those proposed dimensions. The method explicitly permits revision rather than treating an attractive parallel as self-validating. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- This experimentation is presented as a way to remain close to polytheistic factual data. Pausanias, civic calendars, inscriptions, sanctuaries, altars, and sacrificial rules attest plural powers and their associations. [p. 66 / PDF 81]
- Groupings occur throughout the Greek religious landscape, from major Panhellenic sanctuaries to villages. The Delphic temple of Apollo, for example, included an altar to Poseidon. [pp. 66–67 / PDF 81–82]

### Printed p. 67 / PDF 82

- Other Delphic evidence places Gaia and Hestia in the space shared by Apollo and Poseidon. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- In Pharae, a quadrangular bearded Hermes stood beside Hestia’s public hearth altar, and the Hermes–Hestia pair was activated through divination. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- Elsewhere in Pharae, thirty anonymous, unsculpted quadrangular pillars stood in a field. On certain occasions citizens referred to each pillar by a deity’s name. Naming is here situational and ritual, rather than dependent upon permanent iconographic individuality. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- In Aeschylus’s Argos, the fleeing Danaids encounter a hill sanctuary filled with gods. Some powers are recognized directly, including Zeus/Helios and Apollo, while Poseidon and Hermes are identified by trident and wand. The gods share altars. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- The precise arrangement of shared altars remains an open question. Detienne treats possible groupings of two, three, five, and other numbers as analytically consequential rather than incidental. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- The Twelve Gods exemplify local configurational variation. Olympia arranges them as six pairs, while Delos uses four altars of three gods. Other mathematically possible partitions would constitute different relational forms. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- The Erchia calendar joins sacrifice to Semele and Dionysus on the same day and altar, with a goat, a women’s college, the skin allotted to the priestess, and meat consumed on-site. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- In Gamelion, Erchia sacrifices to Poseidon on the same day and in the same place as sacrifices to Zeus Teleios and Hera, inside Hera’s sanctuary. Hera is host and Poseidon is directly associated with the marital pair, leaving “how and why” as the configurational question. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- The altar of Amphiaraos near Oropos organized eighteen deities into five sections. [p. 67 / PDF 82]
- At Claros, Dionysus and Apollo shared the principal altar in Apollo’s major oracular sanctuary. [p. 67 / PDF 82]

### Printed p. 68 / PDF 83

- Greek polytheism materializes in ground plans, altars, temples, sacrificial rules, and figurative representations. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- The evidence consists of established groupings, organized relations, and explicit opposition or complementarity. Structures are empirically manifest at local and Panhellenic scales. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- Pantheons display hierarchies, symmetrical antagonisms, and affinities. Working on these configurations is therefore pragmatic and, in Detienne’s qualified sense, positivist. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- Monographic historians reduce these elementary structures to generic “associations” in the established manner of *Altertumswissenschaft*. Detienne argues that recurrence across a millennium makes the relations analytically primary. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- From Homer through Porphyry, evidence ranges from Olympian statements about authority to Hellenistic civic regulations, dedications, and calendars. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- Detienne describes at least ten centuries of shared gods, sanctuaries, and ritual practices, while explicitly retaining local changes and contextual variation. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- The field has not fully exploited epigraphic discoveries and erudite scholarship that continue to enrich and revise knowledge of pantheons and practice. His critique of specialist framing coexists with reliance on specialist evidence. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
- Histories of Greek religion have privileged origin and etymology over what happens to a deity across sanctuaries and configurations. Detienne redirects attention from first identity toward relational trajectories and transformations. [p. 68 / PDF 83]

### Printed p. 69 / PDF 84

- Detienne does not categorically reject historical explanations of a grouping. He objects to origin-seeking that arbitrarily fixes a deity’s initial “personality” and then describes history as enrichment or impoverishment of that essence. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
- Configurational analysis cannot begin from absolute innocence; the analyst must already possess some idea of Athena’s, Poseidon’s, or Apollo’s outline or manner. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
- Speculations such as Athena as a bundle of fetishes or Apollo as Hittite/violent Asiatic need not prevent an analyst from studying how configurations are organized and how sharply contrasted gods react to one another. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
- Detienne’s preferred safeguard against integral divine personalities is to use concrete objects as “litmus papers.” [p. 69 / PDF 84]
- Objects, gestures, and segments of situations permit analysis of whole relational complexes without allowing implicit individuated characteristics to predetermine the result. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
- In the *mētis* field, useful objects include bit, horse, ship, and rudder. Gestures and actions include guiding, travelling, crossing, and defining limits. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
- Provisional institutional situations include war, marriage, agriculture, death, and birth. Each situation can generate indefinitely many concrete objects and gestures. [pp. 69–70 / PDF 84–85]

### Printed p. 70 / PDF 85

- Detienne’s sample object list includes bow, nightingale, seal, spear, spindle, and winnowing basket. The heterogeneity is intentional: no privileged class of symbols exhausts the field. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- Historians of religion studying divine attributes and symbolisms have already produced useful fragments and conglomerations of representations, even though their organization is not yet relationally adequate. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- Lévi-Straussian ethnology reveals how concrete objects, gestures, and situations deepen interpretation of myths and supernatural figures. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- Any object has, in principle, indefinitely many features and can enter indefinitely many associative series. This creates analytical possibility and a danger of uncontrolled comparison. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- Ethnographic context disciplines that openness. Analysts need fauna, flora, games, hunting, war, material practice, rituals, calendars, religious rules, and native treatises on plants, animals, stones, minerals, and techniques. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- “Plugging into the cultural network” requires encyclopedic contextual knowledge comparable to sustained ethnographic immersion. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- Experimental comparison can select a well-defined, isolable object, observe its different states, and analyse a limited set of variables by reducing their number or holding to variables of one type. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
- Dumézil sometimes privileged similarity, although his work on the warrior function also compared divergent consequences following the Third Hero’s necessary killing. [pp. 70–71 / PDF 85–86]

### Printed p. 71 / PDF 86

- Dumézil found at least four ways in which societies imagined the Third Hero avoiding the consequences of necessary killing. This is a model of comparison that generates difference rather than a single common essence. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- He also used internal interactions and significant details to test the effects of tripartition on concrete and abstract notions or categories considered culturally important. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- A litmus object, gesture, or situation provokes a reaction in contact with a power and can disclose an unnoticed aspect, hidden property, or unexpected angle. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- The minimal experimental question is “what happens” under the selected contact. It does not assume the reaction in advance. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- The horse is historically non-random in an Indo-European civilization shaped by warrior conquest with horse-drawn chariots. Selection of an object must be culturally motivated. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- Attached to a sovereign god’s quadriga, the horse manifests triumphant victory under Jupiter, while Jupiter’s priest is prohibited from riding or sacrificing it. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- Sacrificed ostentatiously to Mars, the horse expresses warrior virtue and death. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- In contact with the productive third function, the horse becomes a member of the equine family and a burden-bearing animal comparable to mule or donkey. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- Detienne’s counterfactual that five functions would yield two further equine qualities shows that the object’s disclosed identity is relationally generated by the classificatory system. [p. 71 / PDF 86]
- The horse’s bit, apparently absent from the Indo-European domain, can be used instead inside Greek configurations involving Athena, Poseidon, the Telchines, Hephaestus, and others. [p. 71 / PDF 86]

### Printed p. 72 / PDF 87

- Microanalysis treats the most concrete details as probes of divine configurations, which Detienne calls the primary data and elementary forms of Greek polytheism. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- Apollo’s epithets, cults, rituals, epic appearances, and Homeric Hymn supply concrete modes of behaviour rather than a single identity statement. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- As Aguieus, Apollo is linked to paths, roads, and networks of ways. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- A seventeen-kilometre sixth-century BCE procession carried stones from Apollo’s Milesian sanctuary to the sanctuary at Didyma, while other stones marked his routes. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- Apollo could have a cult presence as a pillar. The coexistence of mobile stones, fixed route markers, pillar form, and narrative footsteps builds a relational field of spatial organization. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- In the Homeric Hymn, Apollo lays out sites, plants his foot, backtracks, carves territories, and masters path networks. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- The concrete action vocabulary includes setting down a foot, clearing land, placing stones, and founding territories. He moves through *hylē*, the primitive forest later associated with matter. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- Apollo builds roads and, at Delphi, lays foundations, raises walls, and positions a threshold slab. Detienne characterizes him as architect and founder. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- Apollo’s paths intersect Hermes’s paths, Hecate’s crossroads, and Poseidon’s sanctuaries. Stones move and become fixed at specific locations within that crossing network. [p. 72 / PDF 87]
- Thresholds, doors, and precincts introduce a sequence of boundary acts: laying down, crossing, defending, constructing, protecting, plotting, pacing, establishing, and prohibiting. [pp. 72–73 / PDF 87–88]

### Printed p. 73 / PDF 88

- Detienne rejects an evolutionary question that would treat stones or pillars as proof of a transition from aniconic to anthropomorphic representation. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- The Apollo Belvedere, Apollo-pillar, and conical-stone Aguieus coexist. Difference of representational form does not by itself establish a chronological ladder. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- Hermes shares with Apollo qualities involving doors, paths, thresholds, stones, precincts, altars, music, instruments, speech, and effects of speech. Shared field creates the experiment; it does not collapse the gods into one. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- An analyst can obtain concrete orientations without first issuing Apollo a fixed “identity card.” [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- An Apollonian domain is recognized as an overlapping field in which other powers are recurrently or occasionally associated. The domain is graded and relational rather than exclusive. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- The second analytic stage selects a particular configuration and asks why the grouping occurs or what the interconnections signify. Interpretations must be tested against knowledge of polytheisms and evidence about the society using them. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- Delphi gathers Apollo with Poseidon, Hestia, Gaia, Themis, and Dionysus around the Pythian oracle. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- For manageability, the microanalyst generally prefers a pair or triad to a gathering as complex as Delphi’s. This is a methodological reduction, not an assertion that larger configurations are unimportant. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
- The first of two complementary methods internally explores what each member of a pair stands for across stories, cults, rituals, and institutions that produce joint or opposed action. [pp. 73–74 / PDF 88–89]

### Printed p. 74 / PDF 89

- A pair such as Hestia–Hermes or Apollo–Hermes can express a broader pantheon structure through its joint and opposed actions. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- The second method places the pair in contact with other available groupings, beginning with explicit and proximate relations. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- Detienne’s examples include Hestia–Apollo–Poseidon, Hermes–Aphrodite–Hestia, and Poseidon–Gaia–Apollo–Themis. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- Internal pair-analysis and outward configurational testing are complementary procedures for exposing underlying relations. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- Apollo’s Delos-to-Delphi journey can be tracked through objects, gestures, and situations to reconstruct the relations with Gaia, Poseidon, and Hestia. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- *Ktidzein* joins path-clearing through forest to durable establishment or foundation. One action links movement, transformation of terrain, and institution. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- *Bainein* and its compounds link spatial movement with a firm, sure-footed tread. Apollo’s foot trembles the ground and makes a forceful mark. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- Apollo’s straddled stability protects towns and territories. Detienne compares it to a constructed road with an origin and end, *archē* and *telos*. [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- On the journey Apollo encounters Poseidon at Onchestos, where Poseidon governs the newly tamed horse and harnessed chariot. Poseidon’s prior presence matters: he is “already there.” [p. 74 / PDF 89]
- At Delphi, Poseidon supplies Apollo with a pure, flattened base, *dapeda*, on which the founder builds. [pp. 74–75 / PDF 89–90]

### Printed p. 75 / PDF 90

- Apollo places the *themeilia*, threshold slab, and load-bearing walls on Poseidon’s secure setting. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Poseidon’s relevant quality is *asphaleios*, security or stability. In cofounding configurations Apollo acts as *archēgetēs*, while Poseidon is *themeliouchos*, the one who holds foundations fast. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Detienne opposes a scholarly reconstruction of Delphi in which an inherently oracular Earth fits a naturally mantic site of spring, trees, rock cleft, and laurel. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- He invokes a century of excavation that, he says, produced no evidence for Ge’s prophetic activity at Delphi. Ge is mute there and at Olympia. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Rather than an oracular predecessor, marginal Earth represents a permanently secure base corresponding to Poseidon’s pure foundations. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Detienne rejects the generic history-of-religions label *paredros*, “assessor of other deities,” as inflexible and insufficiently precise for this relation. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Ge/Gaia is Euthemethlos, the Well-founded One, self-founded in herself. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- The resulting Delphic hierarchy is relational and architectural: self-founded Earth; Poseidon, establisher and holder of bases; Apollo, builder-founder. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Hestia is related to Earth through her grandmother Rheia and embodies endurance and stability through the household altar-hearth. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- Hestia is both the pure, eternal common fire of Greek cities and a power favouring new cities with their own altars and civic hearth institutions. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
- As Hestia Domatitis, she participates in house construction. [pp. 75–76 / PDF 90–91]

### Printed p. 76 / PDF 91

- Apollo and Poseidon are both Domatites and court Hestia’s unshakeable domestic basis. The configuration joins the founder and foundation-holder through a third power’s stable interior. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- This relation does not make Hestia or Poseidon sources of oracular speech; with respect to the oracle, both remain mute. A configuration is dimension-specific and cannot be generalized across every function. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- Themis, Apollo’s nurse, is separated from the supposed “oracles of Earth” and linked instead to *themistes*, founding words, and *thesmoi*, decrees issued when Apollo speaks. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- Themis mediates between Gaia and Apollo, sits near the tripod, and gives prophetic speech foundational meaning. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- As mother of the Moirai and Horai, Themis links shares/fate, social order, present knowledge, future knowledge, decree, and foundation. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- A different route into the same complex begins with *bainein* and Apollo’s maritime epithets Embasios and Ekbasios, the god who embarks and disembarks. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- Maritime Apollo crosses Poseidon, Athena, and Hera through the Argonauts, oracle consultation, authorization of departure and return, shipbuilding, navigation, and sea peril. [p. 76 / PDF 91]
- Configurational analysis lets the analyst take apart and reassemble what Detienne, citing Clifford Geertz, calls partial logics of thought. [p. 76 / PDF 91]

### Printed p. 77 / PDF 92

- Microanalysis avoids choosing in advance between two totalizing Apollo portraits: Otto’s morally superior god and Defradas’s terribly violent god. Detienne calls such integral portraits robotic. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- Restricting comparison to a small feature, such as foot and threshold in Apollo–Hermes, increases the number of discernible differences between the pair and other powers subsequently introduced. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- Poseidon, Hestia, and Heracles can each be tested against threshold, foot movement, carried stone, or upright fixed stone. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- These manipulations enter small systems of thought embedded in divine gatherings and expose cultural choices and constraints associated with a selected element or rule of action. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- Detienne qualifies his method: repeatable experimentation can revisit and alter conditions, whereas historical phenomena and configurations never recur in precisely the same form. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- “Manipulation” better names the controlled juxtaposition of phenomena and configurations that prompt reactions without literal experimental replication. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- Formal recurrences across a culture’s duration and varied contexts can nevertheless disclose transformations in constituent elements. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
- Detienne expressly preserves the established *schemata* of Olympians, early iconographic individuality, personalized poetic stories and praise, and local or historical redesign of pantheons. [p. 77 / PDF 92]

### Printed p. 78 / PDF 93

- Political assemblies could reorganize festival calendars and sacrificial hierarchies. Such deliberate institutional redesign is important evidence about Greek polytheistic practice. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
- Cities did not combine gods randomly. Political reorganization experimented with combinations made possible by the system. Variation therefore occurs inside structured possibility. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
- Detienne’s method targets unstated relations linking divine powers to many objects and phenomena across social life and the natural world. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
- Myths and Greek “theological” stories can present explicit configurations, but the powers are also joined through prior or wider micronetworks spanning the whole cultural field. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
- Repeated manipulations gradually reveal the richness of the relational fabric. One central result is that every god is plural. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
- Greek islands form only a small part of a vast polytheistic comparative field. Detienne calls for historians and anthropologists to explore it together. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
- The closing commitment to journeys without a definite destination treats comparison as discovery rather than confirmation of a preselected universal. [p. 78 / PDF 93]

## Boundary-making notes — Detienne

1. **Divine jurisdiction is allotted yet porous.** The *timē*/*erga* model assigns a preserve to each power, while actual domains such as marriage and war are populated by many powers with differentiated roles. A boundary marks relational competence and ritual consequence, rather than exclusive occupancy. [pp. 62–63 / PDF 77–78]
2. **A domain boundary is configuration-specific.** Aphrodite’s exclusion from Diomedes’s battle does not erase Ares–Aphrodite at the gates of war. Hestia’s foundational relation at Delphi does not give her an oracular voice. [pp. 63, 76 / PDF 78, 91]
3. **Boundaries are discovered through contrasts.** The analyst maps limits by placing gods in relation, rather than reading a fixed perimeter from an isolated divine personality. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
4. **Spatial boundaries are sequences of acts.** Apollo’s field includes clearing routes, planting feet and stones, laying thresholds, building doors, plotting precincts, pacing borders, defending them, and prohibiting entry. [pp. 72–73 / PDF 87–88]
5. **Crossing is internal to boundary-making.** Paths intersect Hermes, Hecate, and Poseidon, and thresholds exist to be crossed as well as protected. Detienne’s material boundary is therefore an interface that organizes movements and permissions. [pp. 72–73 / PDF 87–88]
6. **Foundation has differentiated layers.** Gaia is self-founded ground, Poseidon holds the stable base, and Apollo builds. The site’s boundary and identity emerge from dependencies among powers. [pp. 74–75 / PDF 89–90]
7. **The household/city boundary links stability and expansion.** Hestia’s fixed hearth supports new civic foundations, while Apollo and Poseidon seek her domestic stability. Interior fixation can enable outward founding. [pp. 75–76 / PDF 90–91]
8. **Analytic boundaries increase resolution.** Restricting comparison to a foot or threshold reveals more distinctions than comparing whole gods. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
9. **Context bounds possible associations.** Because any object has indefinitely many features, cultural and material knowledge must limit which variables and connections are legitimate. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
10. **System boundaries constrain political choice.** Assemblies can alter ritual calendars and sacrificial rank, but the available combinations are not arbitrary. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
11. **Disciplinary boundaries shape what can be seen.** Separating Greek gods from anthropological “crossroads” gods and insulating Greek specialists from comparativism prevents polytheism from appearing as a broad relational field. [pp. 58–59 / PDF 73–74]
12. **Generic labels can harden false boundaries.** The label *paredros* treats Gaia as an assessor beside another god and conceals the more specific foundational relation reconstructed from the configuration. [p. 75 / PDF 90]

## Academia, credentialism, and disciplinary authority — Detienne

1. Detienne presents Greek polytheism as a protected scholarly preserve. “Patented historians” control it by treating Greece as culturally closed and comparison as professionally alien. This is an explicit critique of disciplinary ownership of an object. [p. 58 / PDF 73]
2. The split between anthropological “crossroads gods” and canonical Greek gods reproduces a hierarchy of peoples and evidence. Comparative exclusion is tied to the prestige of Greece, China, India, and “History,” as opposed to societies cast as primitive witnesses. [pp. 57–59 / PDF 72–74]
3. Positivist erudition and spiritual hermeneutics appear opposed, yet both preserve the isolated deity as their unit and therefore miss relational polytheism. Methodological rivalry can conceal a shared ontology. [pp. 58–59 / PDF 73–74]
4. Institutional naming follows intellectual power. Dumézil’s transition from comparative mythology to Indo-European civilization occurs through named chairs, departments, and scholarly circles, showing that classification of a field shapes what becomes legible within it. [p. 59 / PDF 74]
5. Historians who regarded themselves as institutional guarantors of monographic interpretation immediately resisted Detienne’s relational analyses. Detienne explicitly locates the objection in professional guardianship, not solely in evidentiary disagreement. [p. 65 / PDF 80]
6. The individuated boss-god sustains the dissertation monograph. Detienne sarcastically connects its methodological survival to peer approval, career prospects, and a scholar’s confidence from an academic boss. This is the chapter’s most direct credential-and-incentive critique. [p. 64 / PDF 79]
7. The “boss-god” and academic “boss” parallel suggests that hierarchy in the object of study and hierarchy in the institution mutually reinforce attention to central actors while marginalizing lateral services. This parallel is strongly implied by Detienne’s phrasing, although he does not develop it into a formal sociological thesis. [pp. 62, 64 / PDF 77, 79]
8. Established reference scholarship classifies recurrent divine structures as mere “associations.” Detienne calls this lazy because a neutral filing category prevents the grouping itself from becoming an explanatory object. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
9. His critique of disciplinary framing does not reject scholarship. Epigraphy, archaeological evidence, calendars, encyclopedias of attributes, and accumulated erudition are indispensable and still underused. The problem is how evidence is organized. [pp. 68, 70 / PDF 83, 85]
10. Native theologians as “administrators of memory” possess a distinct authority because their statements validate relations and transmit deep linguistic history. The formulation also raises an internal question about whose native speech counts as theology and administration; Detienne reports the Dumézilian assumption without fully examining that selection. [p. 61 / PDF 76]
11. Archaeological authority is contestable when it projects a site’s supposed natural vocation onto the evidence. Detienne opposes the oracular-Gaia reconstruction by invoking the absence of archaeological traces after sustained excavation. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
12. Specialized terminology can substitute classification for analysis. The history-of-religions category *paredros* is criticized as inflexible because it fails to specify Gaia’s exact relation to Poseidon and Apollo. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
13. Origin and etymology attract disciplinary prestige even when they require an arbitrary initial divine personality. Detienne shifts the burden toward observed arrangements, transformations, and coherence within configurations. [pp. 68–69 / PDF 83–84]
14. The chapter’s proposed remedy is collaborative rather than anti-academic: anthropologists and historians should jointly explore polytheistic systems, using the evidence and contextual competence of both. [p. 78 / PDF 93]

## Objections, tensions, and limits internal to or raised by the chapter

### Limits Detienne explicitly recognizes

1. **No presupposition-free analysis:** A configurational analyst still begins with some outline or manner of the gods involved. The method reduces essentialism but cannot eliminate prior identification. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
2. **Historical non-repeatability:** Configurations never recur in precisely the same form, so the procedure lacks literal experimental replication. “Manipulation” is Detienne’s correction. [p. 77 / PDF 92]
3. **Combinatorial excess:** Objects have indefinitely many features and associations. Without deep context, the method could produce arbitrary analogies. [p. 70 / PDF 85]
4. **Scale reduction:** Pairs and triads are methodologically tractable, but Delphi’s larger gathering shows that reducing a complex configuration may omit relations that become visible only at greater scale. Detienne states the preference but does not give a complete protocol for restoring the full complex. [p. 73 / PDF 88]
5. **Relationality does not erase individuality:** Greek gods possess early visual *schemata*, personal stories, local redesigns, and recognizable characteristics. Detienne’s model must account for these without making them primary essences. [pp. 77–78 / PDF 92–93]
6. **History remains admissible:** Detienne does not reject historical causation; he rejects arbitrary origin personalities. Structural and historical explanations must therefore be coordinated rather than treated as mutually exclusive. [p. 69 / PDF 84]
7. **Results remain revisable:** A proposed reaction needs further manipulations that can confirm, correct, or reject it. One evocative configuration is not sufficient validation. [p. 66 / PDF 81]

### Further critical tensions — analytic inference from Detienne’s text

1. **Native statement versus unstated network:** Dumézilian validation privileges explicit native theology, while Detienne’s mature method emphasizes implicit material micronetworks. The chapter does not fully specify how conflicts between explicit theology and material configuration should be adjudicated. [pp. 61, 78 / PDF 76, 93]
2. **“Already there” versus analytic construction:** Detienne calls structures primary data already present in altars and rituals, but the analyst selects variables, narrows the field, and creates novel juxtapositions. Observed grouping and analyst-produced manipulation need separate evidence labels. [pp. 60, 70–71, 77 / PDF 75, 85–86, 92]
3. **Mode of action paradox:** The question “how rather than where” is designed to escape crude domain labels, yet a constant, deity-specific mode becomes another essence. The chapter convincingly diagnoses this regression but offers no single replacement identity rule beyond iterative positional plurality. [pp. 61, 64 / PDF 76, 79]
4. **Constraint can become circular:** Political combinations are said to be system-permitted rather than arbitrary. Unless the permissible system is inferred independently of the observed combinations, evidence and constraint could define each other circularly. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
5. **Long duration versus transformation:** Detienne invokes ten centuries of the “same” gods, sanctuaries, and rituals while emphasizing local variation and reconfiguration. The identity criterion allowing something to count as the same across transformation remains deliberately loose. [p. 68 / PDF 83]
6. **Greek evidence and larger claims:** “Every god is plural” is argued from Greek configurations, while the final vast polytheistic continent is proposed as future comparative work. The cross-cultural generality is a research program, not a demonstrated result within this chapter. [p. 78 / PDF 93]
7. **Contextual expertise and authority:** Encyclopedic cultural competence constrains fanciful association, but reliance on highly trained specialists may reproduce some of the authority structures Detienne criticizes. His actual position seems to require specialist knowledge without specialist ownership. [pp. 58, 68, 70, 78 / PDF 73, 83, 85, 93]
8. **Negative portraits may be compressed:** Nilsson, Otto, Defradas, origin historians, archaeologists, and monographers are often represented through sharply simplified positions. Their strongest counterarguments would need to be read directly before treating Detienne’s characterizations as complete. [pp. 58–59, 68–69, 75, 77 / PDF 73–74, 83–84, 90, 92]
9. **Archaeological absence:** Detienne treats a century without evidence for prophetic Ge as decisive against the reconstruction. Absence after intensive excavation is relevant, but the claim should remain tied to the evidentiary state and date of the book rather than converted into an unrestricted proof of nonexistence. [p. 75 / PDF 90]
10. **Lexical mediation:** Much of the Apollo analysis depends on semantic families such as *ktidzein*, *bainein*, *themistes*, and *thesmoi*. The argument requires philological validation of translations, semantic range, chronology, and distribution beyond the chapter’s compressed presentation. [pp. 74–76 / PDF 89–91]
11. **Source density varies:** Pages 57–72 have 28 notes supporting intellectual history and examples, but the detailed Delphi reconstruction on pp. 72–78 is covered mainly by one blanket reference to Detienne’s 1998 Apollo study. Individual claims should be traced there before external publication. [p. 72 n. 28 / PDF 87; notes pp. 107–108 / PDF 122–123]
12. **The method risks endlessness:** The closing “journeys with no definite destination” protects discovery from predetermined universals, while offering no stopping rule for saturation, sufficient confirmation, or complete coverage. [p. 78 / PDF 93]

## Provisional Polymyth relevance — inference, not Detienne’s claims or project rulings

1. **Use configurations as an analytic unit.** A Polymyth study could begin from attested groupings of figures, variants, rituals, objects, scenes, and institutions, then treat the isolated character profile as one derived view rather than the primary object. This follows Detienne’s shift from individuated deity to relational configuration. [Inference from pp. 59–60, 68 / PDF 74–75, 83]
2. **Preserve plurality across appearances.** The same figure may occupy different and even apparently opposed positions across works, communities, scenes, or periods. Those positions should be mapped before assigning a constant identity. [Inference from pp. 60–64, 78 / PDF 75–79, 93]
3. **Separate field from mode, then resist re-essentialization.** It may be useful to record both where a figure operates and how it operates, while treating any “constant mode” as a hypothesis open to counter-configurations. [Inference from pp. 61, 64, 66 / PDF 76, 79, 81]
4. **Start from evidence that is already relational.** Shared scenes, paired invocations, joint altars, common objects, ritual sequences, and explicit oppositions are stronger starting points than abstract thematic similarity. [Inference from pp. 60, 66–68 / PDF 75, 81–83]
5. **Distinguish observed grouping from analytical juxtaposition.** A source-attested pair, a reconstructed relation, and a deliberately created comparison should not be stored as the same evidence type. Detienne’s chapter moves among all three. [Inference from pp. 60, 66, 70–71, 77 / PDF 75, 81, 85–86, 92]
6. **Use concrete probes.** Objects, gestures, spatial acts, and narrow situation segments can provide disciplined comparison variables. A threshold or foot can disclose differences that a broad label such as “god of roads” erases. [Inference from pp. 69–77 / PDF 84–92]
7. **Narrowing can increase resolution.** Restricting a comparison to one feature and a few variables should be treated as a deliberate analytic pass, with later reintegration into the larger configuration. [Inference from pp. 70, 73, 77 / PDF 85, 88, 92]
8. **Context is a validity condition.** Because objects support limitless associations, a comparison requires cultural knowledge of materials, ecology, technique, institutions, language, ritual, and local history. Formal similarity alone cannot validate meaning. [Inference from p. 70 / PDF 85]
9. **Build correction into the method.** A proposed relation should generate further tests across neighboring configurations that can confirm, qualify, or reject it. [Inference from p. 66 / PDF 81]
10. **Record local arrangement, not just membership.** The same set can be organized into pairs, triads, hosts, guests, shared altars, marginal figures, or hierarchies. Set membership alone loses the relation that creates meaning. [Inference from p. 67 / PDF 82]
11. **Model domains as overlapping and role-differentiated.** A domain such as war, marriage, travel, foundation, or speech can be jointly populated. Presence outside a figure’s usual field need not be an inconsistency if the configuration gives it a distinct aspect. [Inference from pp. 62–63, 76 / PDF 77–78, 91]
12. **Treat absence as contextual evidence.** A figure’s exclusion from one scene may reflect the currently activated configuration, rather than a universal limit. [Inference from p. 63 / PDF 78]
13. **Represent boundaries as actions and interfaces.** Detienne’s threshold material suggests recording who establishes, crosses, defends, protects, paces, or prohibits a boundary, and in relation to whom. A generic “boundary theme” would lose these differences. [Inference from pp. 72–73 / PDF 87–88]
14. **Do not convert representational difference into a developmental ladder without evidence.** Coexisting pillar, stone, anthropomorphic, narrative, and ritual forms may be simultaneous modes rather than successive stages. [Inference from p. 73 / PDF 88]
15. **Retain history without origin essentialism.** Diachronic change should be tracked as movement among configurations, while avoiding an arbitrary “original true personality” against which later variants are judged. [Inference from pp. 68–69 / PDF 83–84]
16. **Treat institutional rearrangement as mythic practice.** Calendar revision, sacrificial rank, canon formation, curriculum, archive structure, and other organized choices may actively reconfigure a mythic system while remaining constrained by inherited possibilities. [Inference from p. 78 / PDF 93]
17. **Audit scholarly categories for unit bias.** Credentialed fields may contain excellent evidence while organizing it around career-safe monographs, canonical individuals, or inherited labels. A Polymyth method could preserve the evidence while rechecking the unit of analysis. [Inference from pp. 58, 64–65, 68, 70 / PDF 73, 79–80, 83, 85]
18. **Avoid turning institutional critique into wholesale dismissal.** Detienne depends on Pausanias, inscriptions, archaeology, philology, reference works, and accumulated erudition. His critique targets enclosure and framing, while his method requires deep scholarship. [Inference from pp. 66–70, 75 / PDF 81–85, 90]
19. **Do not canonize the chapter’s terms automatically.** “Micronetwork,” “litmus paper,” “manipulation,” “partial logic,” and “every god is plural” are productive prompts, but they should remain sourced concepts until tested against current Polymyth definitions and evidence. [Inference from pp. 69–71, 76–78 / PDF 84–86, 91–93]
20. **The chapter supports a meaning-first warning for formal systems.** A schema can record positions, pairs, variables, and evidence, but cultural interpretation determines which features are salient and which relations are legitimate. [Inference from pp. 69–71 / PDF 84–86]
21. **A stopping rule remains needed.** Detienne supplies iterative discovery and correction but no criterion for when a configurational analysis is saturated or complete. Any Polymyth adoption would need to solve that operational limit without pretending that formal completion equals semantic completion. [Inference from pp. 66, 78 / PDF 81, 93]
22. **Cross-cultural transfer remains provisional.** The chapter’s worked demonstration is Greek. Its final invitation to a broader polytheistic continent licenses comparative research, not immediate universalization of the Greek findings. [Inference from p. 78 / PDF 93]
