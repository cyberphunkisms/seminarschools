### Printed p. 40 [PDF 55]

#### Source content

- **S40.01 | Opening thesis:** Many contemporary people treat being part of history and possessing a past as self-evident, although societies differ sharply in whether they experience their past and historical consciousness as secure. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **S40.02 | Case, ex-Soviet Russia:** Soviet society had an official history that called itself scientific while being repeatedly rewritten to fit Communist Party ideology and Marxist interpretive schemes. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **S40.03 | Boundary, credentialed knowledge:** Detienne places "scientific" in scare quotes while describing an official history repeatedly altered according to ideological criteria and Party requirements. (p. 40 [PDF 55])

- **S40.05 | Suppressed counter-history:** Russians condemned the official record's lies and began writing formerly prohibited histories of concentration camps, purges, and nocturnal executions. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **S40.06 | Re-periodization:** By 1989, criticism extended to the October Revolution, while some sought continuity with the industrialized and westernized Russia that preceded 1917. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **S40.07 | Past-future relation:** For the new liberal elite, recovery of a pre-revolutionary past promised a better future. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **S40.08 | Competing memory response:** Awareness of collective culpability led part of the population toward forgetting Stalinism rather than publicly incorporating it. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **S40.09 | Identity problem:** For most Russians, constructing an acceptable image of themselves in the past became both urgent and difficult. This sentence continues on p. 41. (pp. 40-41 [PDF 55-56])

#### Provisional Polymyth relevance, AI inference

- **P40.01 | Historical regimes as contested constructions:** A mythic or historical corpus should be examined for rival pasts produced after institutional rupture, including official, suppressed, restorative, culpability-centered, and forgetful versions. (Inference from p. 40 [PDF 55])
- **P40.02 | Credentialism mechanism:** Institutional claims such as "scientific" can operate as boundary credentials while content remains politically rewritten. - **P40.04 | Non-unitary reception:** Regime change does not produce one liberated memory. It can generate incompatible projects of disclosure, restoration, guilt, and forgetting. (Inference from p. 40 [PDF 55])

### Printed p. 42 [PDF 57]

#### Source content

- **S42.01 | Name and disciplinary debate:** Claude Lévi-Strauss revisited the older history-ethnology dispute in a 1983 Marc Bloch Lecture addressed to professional historians. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.02 | Lévi-Strauss's paradox:** He argued that all societies are equally historical, while some openly recognize historicity and others prefer to ignore it. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.03 | Lévi-Strauss's formulation:** He also said that societies then labeled primitive were wary of history even while subjected to it. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.04 | Key distinction, two meanings of historical:** One meaning is existing in natural time like other living beings. The other is awareness that humanity possesses a history which it makes or appropriates. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.05 | Detienne's objection:** Lévi-Strauss's paradox implies an overly simple and voluntary choice between recognizing historicity and concealing it. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.06 | Evolutionary boundary criticized:** Detienne reads Lévi-Strauss as elegantly valorizing societies once called archaic and mythical, yet still imagining a transition in which their conceptual self-reflection withdraws before philosophy as a precondition of science. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.07 | Western historical difficulty:** Even in the West, conceiving the past as other than the present, perhaps radically separated from it, was a long and difficult intellectual development. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.08 | Scientific chronology is historically recent:** Objective temporal calculation based on physical, mathematical, astronomical, and calendrical time is a relatively recent acquisition in Western culture. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.09 | Negative definition of historical self-recognition:** A society need not use linear time to recognize itself as historical. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.10 | Negative definition continued:** Historical self-recognition does not require privileging unpredictable, unique, nonrepeating events. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.11 | Negative definition continued:** It does not require discovering a dynamism of human history. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
- **S42.12 | Contemporary impetus:** Global upheavals and demands associated with multiculturalism invite anthropologists and historians to compare diverse ways of experiencing history across space and time. This sentence concludes on p. 43. (pp. 42-43 [PDF 57-58])

- **P42.02 | Anti-developmental safeguard:** Myth, philosophy, science, and historiography should not be placed on a single ladder in which one must disappear for the next to begin. - **P42.03 | Multiple temporal logics:** Cyclical, recursive, genealogical, ritual, cosmic, or event-centered time can all support forms of historical self-relation. Linear chronology cannot operate as an automatic coherence test. (Inference from p. 42 [PDF 57])

1. **Being historical versus recognizing historicity:** Existing through time and change differs from consciously constructing or appropriating a history. (pp. 42-43 [PDF 57-58])
2. **Memory versus representation of a past:** Memory does not automatically produce a past represented as an object. (pp. 43-45 [PDF 58-60])
3. **Memory versus historiographical inquiry:** A memory can found conduct, identity, covenant, or ancestry without investigating a past in itself. (pp. 45, 50 [PDF 60, 65])
4. **Stored information versus temporal self-distance:** Memory management can concern distance between a present self and another temporal self rather than control of an informational stock. (p. 44 [PDF 59])
5. **Undergoing change versus recognizing change:** Radical upheaval can occur without collective theorization of it. (p. 46 [PDF 61])
6. **Change versus progress:** Sophisticated models of change do not entail infinite progress or a dynamic of history. (pp. 46-47 [PDF 61-62])
7. **Future orientation versus linear time:** Roman futurity and destiny can expand without modern linear chronology. (pp. 49-50 [PDF 64-65])
8. **Unique contingent event versus meaningful ordered event:** Modern historicism privileges singular unpredictability; Chinese annalistic interpretation places events in moral-cosmic evolution. (pp. 47-49 [PDF 62-64])
9. **Past as living authority versus past as autonomous other:** Ancestors, epic, and *memoria* keep the past present, while modern historicism tries to separate what has been from what is. (pp. 50-54 [PDF 65-69])
10. **Oldest surviving document versus earliest historical discourse:** An artifact's evidentiary value to modern historians does not establish its original purpose as history. (p. 51 [PDF 66])
11. **Writing versus criticism:** Writing is one critical technology among possible ritual, oral, performative, and counter-narrative operations. (pp. 46, 55 [PDF 61, 70])
12. **Truth selection versus variant collation:** Hecataeus selects what seems true; Japanese court philology preserves variants side by side. (pp. 55-56 [PDF 70-71])
13. **Textual policy versus production authority:** Variant plurality or oral transcription can coexist with complete state control of the historiographical project. (p. 56 [PDF 71])
14. **Tradition as continuity versus criticism of tradition:** Homer organizes continuity, while Hecataeus, Herodotus, ritual debate, and counter-stories display different critical operations. (pp. 53-56 [PDF 68-71])
15. **One culture versus one regime:** Detienne explicitly denies necessary coherence and a close culture-regime correspondence. (p. 43 [PDF 58])
16. **Internal intelligibility versus external recognition:** Indigenous signs and myths can be socially legible yet fail to possess the evidentiary credentials recognized by Western courts. (p. 41 [PDF 56])
17. **Official status versus epistemic independence:** State, party, court, priesthood, and palace positions can authorize historical work while constraining what may be written. (pp. 40-41, 48-49, 56 [PDF 55-56, 63-64, 71])
18. **Canon as resource versus canon as obstacle:** Homer supplies shared knowledge and evidence while constraining the separation of past from present. (pp. 53-54 [PDF 68-69])

## Academic, disciplinary, and credential-boundary notes

The points below synthesize source content specifically relevant to credentialism and academia. They remain page-cited so they can be reused independently.

1. **Official scientific branding:** Soviet history's self-description as scientific did not prevent ideological rewriting. Institutional certification and epistemic independence diverged. (p. 40 [PDF 55])
2. **Court-defined evidence:** Judges required Indigenous claims to enter a Western historical-proof regime structured by lawyers, state archives, and centrally authorized documents. (p. 41 [PDF 56])
3. **Professional audience:** Lévi-Strauss framed the history-ethnology problem before professional historians, foregrounding a dispute over disciplinary jurisdiction. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
4. **History-ethnology division:** The inherited assignment of some societies to ethnology rather than history helped produce the false with-history/without-history binary. (pp. 42-43, 55 [PDF 57-58, 70])
5. **Category co-production:** Historians and anthropologists must adjust to one another rather than allowing either discipline's categories to rule comparison in advance. (p. 43 [PDF 58])
6. **Mission-seeking historians:** Detienne criticizes historians who generate a professional mission by declaring that authentic memory is disappearing. (p. 44 [PDF 59])
7. **Disciplinary operationalization of memory:** Philosophers, historians of memory, and laboratory cognitivists privilege different objects, temporal self-distance versus information storage. (p. 44 [PDF 59])
8. **Comparative canon neglect:** Historians' lack of interest in Vedic and Hindu mnemonic practices constrains what appears in the scholarly comparison. (p. 44 [PDF 59])
9. **Exclusive school branding:** Comparative psychological history called itself the only truly Marxist psychology, an explicit exclusivity claim, while remaining little known among anthropologists. (p. 45 [PDF 60])
10. **Writing bias:** Historians' focus on societies with abundant written technique limits knowledge of nonwritten spaces of criticism. (p. 46 [PDF 61])
11. **Prestige through exceptional object:** Thucydides uses unprecedented war as the epistemic basis for claiming superiority to predecessors in history. (p. 47 [PDF 62])
12. **Official historian lineage:** Chinese historians derive their institutional role from diviner-scribes and operate as imperial officials, combining ritual, bureaucratic, astronomical, and calendrical expertise. (p. 48 [PDF 63])
13. **State monopoly:** Chinese annalistic history remained a state affair for more than two millennia in Detienne's account. (p. 48 [PDF 63])
14. **Priestly credential:** Roman rank, public calendar control, custody of selected events, and ritual decision-making join official office to temporal authority. (p. 49 [PDF 64])
15. **Modern documentary projection:** Turkish funerary stelae are foundational documents for modern historians, but that credential does not establish that their makers were practicing historiography. (p. 51 [PDF 66])
16. **Colonial role conversion:** Mossi griots become the candidates best qualified for the imported historian role because their existing work is semimemorial and seminarrative. The new credential transforms rather than merely recognizes the prior function. (p. 51 [PDF 66])
17. **Colonial obligation to history:** White conquerors bring writing and require a group to produce history as the basis of identity. This makes historical identity an imposed administrative and epistemic demand. (p. 51 [PDF 66])
18. **Educational canon:** Homer serves as the basis of literacy education and the standard for cultural knowledge, giving epic institutional authority over later genealogy and history. (p. 53 [PDF 68])
19. **Disciplinary object created by exclusion:** Herodotus forms a human recent past as a historical object by excluding gods, heroes, Homer, and Hesiod. (pp. 54-55 [PDF 69-70])
20. **State-commissioned authorship:** Japanese and Chinese writers produce rival textual forms under imperial decree and palace administration, limiting authorial choice despite philological sophistication. (p. 56 [PDF 71])
21. **Imported expertise and official service:** Chinese court writers possess a longer historiographical training but remain officials obeying central authority. Expertise, credential, and autonomy do not coincide. (p. 56 [PDF 71])
22. **Independent-scholar contrast:** Hecataeus appears as a citizen writing at leisure and choosing among variants without state control, although Detienne does not analyze the social conditions enabling that freedom. (p. 56 [PDF 71])

## Source-stated objections, evidentiary limits, and unresolved hypotheses

1. Lévi-Strauss's recognize-or-conceal alternative treats relation to historicity as too simple and voluntary. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
2. Linear time, singular events, and historical dynamism are not necessary conditions of historical self-recognition. (p. 42 [PDF 57])
3. Comparative analysis must avoid immediate value judgment, rigid typology, presumed coherence, and one-culture-one-regime mappings. (p. 43 [PDF 58])
4. Memory does not spontaneously create a representation of the past, and "true memory" is not the possession of romanticized societies without history. (pp. 43-44 [PDF 58-59])
5. The proposed relation between individual memory, collective memory, and common past is a neglected hypothesis rather than a settled fact. (p. 45 [PDF 60])
6. The claim that historical thought requires present-past distance is explicitly presented as experimental and hypothetical. (p. 45 [PDF 60])
7. The proposition that memory does not automatically generate historical thought requires firm testing. (p. 45 [PDF 60])
8. Nonwritten spaces of criticism probably exist, but the available knowledge of them is limited. (p. 46 [PDF 61])
9. Experiencing upheaval does not demonstrate awareness or theory of change. (p. 46 [PDF 61])
10. Greek analysis of change does not establish an ancient doctrine of infinite progress or nineteenth-century historical dynamics. (p. 47 [PDF 62])
11. The past-in-itself is historically produced by modern Western thought and should be defamiliarized through comparison. (p. 47 [PDF 62])
12. Roman Pontifex maximus annual posting is described with evidentiary caution, "seems to have made a habit." (p. 49 [PDF 64])
13. Turkish funerary eulogies are early written evidence for modern historians but do not therefore constitute early historical discourse. (p. 51 [PDF 66])
14. Mossi royal funeral narration does not, for Detienne, demonstrate a past experienced as both present and having-been. (p. 51 [PDF 66])
15. Vernant's Homeric funeral-epic origin scene is attractive but reconstructed, archaic rather than primitive. (p. 52 [PDF 67])
16. Direct contact between surviving epic and funeral ritual appears lost. (p. 52 [PDF 67])
17. Whether eighth- or seventh-century epic poets served as group social memory cannot be known from the available evidence. (p. 52 [PDF 67])
18. Apparent temporal separation in a modern reading of epic is contested by Greek vocabulary, canon formation, and the operation of Homeric continuity. (pp. 52-54 [PDF 67-69])
19. The capacity for societies to work critically on traditions is posed as a broad anthropological question; forms of reflexivity need case-specific observation. (p. 55 [PDF 70])
20. The field of comparative historicities remains largely unexplored and depends on sustained historian-anthropologist collaboration. (p. 56 [PDF 71])

## Additional analytical tensions to preserve, AI inference

These are criticisms or cautions generated from the chapter and are not statements Detienne explicitly ratifies.

1. **Anti-hierarchy versus the past-in-itself threshold:** Detienne rejects societies-with-history versus societies-without-history, yet repeatedly treats autonomous interest in a past that "is and has been" as rare and difficult. The chapter does not fully resolve whether this is a neutral difference or a privileged developmental achievement. (Inference from pp. 41-45, 50-54 [PDF 56-60, 65-69])
2. **Anti-essentialism versus civilizational compression:** The method denies close culture-regime correspondence, yet examples are frequently organized as "China," "Rome," "India," "Israel," "black Africa," and "Greece," sometimes across centuries or millennia. The scale of the examples can obscure internal plurality. (Inference from pp. 43-50 [PDF 58-65])
3. **Suspending typology versus repeated criteria:** The chapter asks comparison to suspend immediate typology while organizing cases through self-distance, critical space, awareness of change, linearity, event singularity, and past-otherness. The transition from open comparison to these recurring criteria remains underexplained. (Inference from pp. 43-51 [PDF 58-66])
4. **Myth as valid historical medium versus myth as fiction:** Indigenous mythical accounts expose Western evidentiary exclusion, and Amerindian myth becomes history, yet Herodotus's achievement is described through separation from mythical fictions. The term "myth" changes function across cases and requires more explicit control. (Inference from pp. 41, 51-55 [PDF 56, 66-70])
5. **Critique of documentary projection versus lexical projection:** Detienne correctly refuses to infer historiography from stelae, but his inference from the absence of a Greek noun for the past may also need corroboration beyond vocabulary. (Inference from pp. 51-53 [PDF 66-68])
6. **State control versus interpretive heterogeneity:** Chinese and Japanese historiography are described as state affairs, but the chapter offers little evidence here about dissent, unofficial practices, internal conflict, or transformation within state institutions. (Inference from pp. 48, 56 [PDF 63, 71])
7. **Greek autonomy idealized:** The final contrast describes Hecataeus and other Greek citizens as free of control by city or social body, leaving the material conditions and exclusions of citizen leisure unanalyzed. (Inference from p. 56 [PDF 71])
8. **Colonial-language residue:** Terms such as primitive, native, black Africa, and White conquerors belong partly to the objects criticized and partly to the book's own early-2000s anthropological register. Current use should specify communities and speakers more precisely. (Inference from pp. 42, 50-52 [PDF 57, 65-67])
9. **Historical accuracy versus regime description:** A regime can be described through how it makes truth, time, and authority, but that does not decide whether its particular claims are factually accurate. Polymyth should keep semantic coherence, social function, and factual validation separate. (Inference from the chapter as a whole)
10. **Comparison under asymmetrical archives:** Detienne calls for comparison while demonstrating that court, state, script, colonialism, and disciplinary selection create unequal evidence. The method needs a way to compare without treating archival abundance as conceptual superiority. (Inference from pp. 41, 46, 48-56 [PDF 56, 61, 63-71])

## Provisional Polymyth extraction, AI inference

The chapter supports the following candidate questions for later project use. These are prompts, not ratified Polymyth criteria or canonical terminology.

1. What relation does the narrative establish among past, present, and future?
2. Does the past remain a living authority, become an autonomous other, recur ritually, disclose cosmic order, legitimate identity, diagnose the present, or promise a future?
3. Who is authorized to narrate the past: ruler, state official, court, priest, diviner, lawyer, archivist, bard, griot, storyteller, citizen writer, witness, ancestor, or community?
4. Which institution recognizes the account, and which communities already understand it without that institution's recognition?
5. What counts as evidence within the account: archive, state document, sign, mark, genealogy, territorial memory, ritual, epic, myth, witness, variant, calendar, prodigy, artifact, or divine-human correspondence?
6. Which evidence formats are excluded or forced into translation by courts, states, disciplines, colonial authorities, or academic methods?
7. Is the account written, oral, performed, ritualized, monumental, calendrical, material, or mixed across media?
8. Does surviving medium match original function, or is a modern discipline repurposing an artifact as evidence?
9. Does the society or narrative merely undergo change, explicitly recognize it, theorize it, deliberate over it, will it, or use change to reveal a more general human or cosmic structure?
10. Is time linear, cyclical, recursive, genealogical, cosmic-moral, calendrical, event-centered, destiny-shaped, or multiply organized?
11. Are events singular and contingent, repetitions within an order, signs of hidden meaning, measures of present grandeur, or reasons for ritual intervention?
12. How does the work handle variants: select one as true, place all side by side, synthesize, stage ritual comparison, answer with a counter-story, or leave contradiction open?
13. Does criticism occur through explicit prose, choice among variants, ritual sequencing, counter-narration, semantic innovation, or institutional break?
14. Does a canon teach the medium of thought, define cultural knowledge, preserve continuity, supply evidence, constrain difference, or perform several of these at once?
15. Does the account separate divine and human time, or make ancestors, gods, heroes, and living people temporally continuous?
16. Is historical production independent, commissioned, bureaucratic, legally compelled, colonially imposed, pedagogically canonical, or professionally credentialed?
17. What future does the past authorize: restoration, progress, destiny, expiation, identity repair, territorial restitution, political reform, guilt, or forgetting?
18. Does the culture or corpus exhibit multiple incompatible historicity regimes? If so, the plurality should be retained rather than repaired into assumed coherence.
19. How widely is the model recognized and how is it received? Recognition, popularity, official authorization, and internal coherence should remain separate.
20. Which claims about historical consciousness are demonstrated, which are reconstructed, which are hypotheses needing tests, and which remain unknowable?
