## I. Disciplinary opening and the case for deliberately difficult comparison

- Joint work between historians and anthropologists should appear institutionally easy because historical research centres gather many kinds of historians while anthropological laboratories already span multiple regions and continents. [printed p. 22]
- Detienne contrasts the comparative reach of Indianists, Africanists, and Americanists studying systems of thought with the narrower horizon of a scholar confined to one century. The point concerns scale and comparative possibility, rather than the intrinsic value of seventeenth-century study. [printed p. 22]
- Historians who escape competitive national histories already practice comparison, although their comparisons tend to be socioeconomic more often than cultural. [printed p. 22]
- Detienne’s enabling premise is that, in broad methodological terms, studying societies distant in space and studying societies distant in time do not constitute fundamentally separate intellectual operations. [printed p. 22]
- He identifies Bernard S. Cohn’s 1980 discussion of an anthropologist among historians as a rare explicit endorsement of that premise. [printed p. 22]
- The obstacle is disciplinary and identity-protective prejudice. Detienne suggests that claims of specificity become especially forceful when a scholar’s or discipline’s identity feels threatened. [printed pp. 22–23]
- One recurring historical objection says that comparison is legitimate only after objects have already been shown to be comparable. Detienne treats this as a circular restriction because it denies comparison the power to construct the relevant relation. [printed pp. 22–23]
- Ethnologists are not exempt from defensive particularism. They may invoke ownership of a field or the total originality of a culture to protect a local scholarly domain. [printed p. 23]
- Working together requires scholars to loosen their attachment to what is geographically, culturally, disciplinarily, or biographically nearest to them. [printed p. 23]
- The desired field includes every human society and every possible or imaginable community, with historians and anthropologists intellectually intermingled rather than assigned separate temporal and spatial territories. [printed p. 23]
- Detienne rejects the inherited advice to compare only neighbouring societies, societies that supposedly developed in parallel, societies placed at the same “level” of civilization, or societies already displaying conspicuous similarities. [printed p. 23]
- The Prague linguistic laboratory of the 1930s supplies his counterexample. Its scholars deliberately compared unrelated languages with dissimilar structures instead of awaiting epistemological permission. [printed p. 23]
- Detienne presents phonology as one result of this willingness to experiment across prior classifications of relation and similarity. [printed p. 23]
- The contrastive benefit of joint historical-anthropological work is cognitive dissonance: a distant configuration can expose a feature that specialists and earlier observers failed to notice in a familiar one. [printed p. 23]
- Karl Polanyi’s collaborative work at Columbia is offered as an example of ethnologists and historians jointly questioning the status and assumptions of ancient economies, ancient civilizations, and modern economies. [printed p. 23]
- Another example is using knowledge of India to reconsider how salt was administered in the Ottoman Empire and France. The direction of illumination runs from a field conventionally treated as distant back toward familiar historical fields. [printed p. 23]
- A further comparison asks how a Greek philosopher and a Chinese sage differ within a deliberately restricted configuration and from which angles the difference becomes visible. [printed p. 23]

## II. The singular-plural comparativist and a collaborative procedure

- Constructive comparison adopts the widest possible field of cultural representations, including distant and nearby societies, past societies, and living groups observed both recently and historically. [printed p. 24]
- The comparativist must be able to move analytically among the French Revolutionary Constituent Assembly, southern Ethiopia, the European Commission, early Greek cities, and medieval assemblies in Siena or Verona. The point is freedom from national, period, and civilizational passports. [printed p. 24]
- Detienne’s comparativist is simultaneously singular and plural. Comparison requires a responsible analyst, yet the requisite knowledge is distributed among collaborators. [printed p. 24]
- A single polymath can sometimes cover a bounded comparative domain. Georges Dumézil’s individual study of Indo-European civilization is Detienne’s example. [printed p. 24]
- Once the field is expanded to the range of human societies, solitary polymathy ceases to be a credible procedure. [printed p. 24]
- The operational unit Detienne proposes is a network of approximately three or four historian-anthropologists or anthropologist-historians. [printed p. 24]
- Each participant should absorb the others’ knowledge and questions while continuing to provide close analysis of the society or civilization for which that participant began as the professional interpreter. [printed p. 24]
- Disciplinary expertise remains necessary, although it becomes an input to collective analysis instead of a proprietary boundary around a culture. [printed p. 24]
- The collaborative network should function as a microgroup of colleagues or accomplices who are prepared to think aloud together. [printed p. 24]
- A regular meeting place matters more to this method than a large research grant because recurring shared thought produces the competence of a historical-anthropological microcommunity. [printed p. 24]
- The minimum viable group may contain one historian and one anthropologist if each genuinely participates in the other’s questions, knowledge, curiosity, and competence. [printed p. 24]
- Detienne bases his practical recommendations on retrospective analysis of ten years of collaborative work that had produced four comparative volumes and recently completed a fifth. [printed p. 24]
- His primary worked example is the 1990 project *Tracés de fondation*, translated in the chapter as “Paths leading to foundations.” [printed pp. 24–25]
- That project began through reciprocal curiosity: a Hellenist studied Africanist accounts of ritual and representational ways of constructing territory, while an ethnologist studied a Hellenist’s account of a god whose attributed actions produced a political territory across many small Greek cities over four centuries. [printed p. 25]
- The collaboration reopened the basic question, “What is a site?” rather than beginning with a settled definition of territory. [printed p. 25]
- One provoking comparison paired an Africanist configuration, in which village space required clearing bush, with a Brahmin Indian configuration, in which the village determined its boundaries rather than being determined by pre-existing boundaries. [printed p. 25]
- Placing those cases together destabilized the apparent self-evidence of bush, village, and boundary and shifted attention toward multiple procedures of territorialization. [printed p. 25]

## III. Selecting a productive category and receiving the “shock” of incomparability

- To enter the variety of territorialization practices, the group needed a starting category broad enough to open comparison, yet neither so general that it became empty nor so tied to one culture that it dictated the result. [printed p. 25]
- The selected category was the cluster “founding, foundations, founders.” [printed p. 25]
- Responses from Africanists, Japan specialists, Americanists, and Hellenists showed that the category was complex but productive because it generated a connected field of questions. [printed p. 25]
- A category that classifies too powerfully blocks discovery by forcing cases into its pre-existing divisions. [printed p. 25]
- A category that is too weak supplies no common object for collective thought, even if its ordinary language seems loosely to cover many beginnings and inaugurations. [printed p. 25]
- The initial category therefore functions as a calibrated probe, not as a definition to be defended at all costs. [printed p. 25]
- The group could move from conspicuous acts of foundation centred on an identifiable founder toward journeys, processes, and ritual gestures occurring before or apart from a discrete founding act. [printed pp. 25–26]
- The decisive methodological event was the discovery of a case that appeared incomparable under the starting category. [printed p. 26]
- Two Japan specialists, after a prolonged silence during the group’s exploratory work, reported with discomfort that the oldest Japanese texts contained neither a founding nor a founder. [printed p. 26]
- Detienne treats their negative finding as a success. It makes possible a genuine inquiry into what durable establishment or foundation has been assumed to mean. [printed p. 26]
- Incomparability clouds, fractures, and disassembles the familiar category instead of excluding the resistant case. [printed p. 26]
- The method does not ask how to make Japan resemble a familiar foundation story. It asks which hidden components of “foundation” Japan’s resistance has exposed. [printed p. 26]
- Japan and Vedic India become principal examples of societies whose surviving representations resist the idea of founding. [printed p. 26]

## IV. Japan, Vedic India, and the concealed components of “foundation”

- Detienne characterizes the Japanese configuration as continuity extending from primordial time rather than an inaugural break followed by historical duration. [printed p. 26]
- In the account he summarizes, celestial gods and autochthonous gods jointly order the world. The autochthonous side includes civilized masters of the land who become marriage partners for celestial gods and wild autochthonous beings displaced to the underworld or sea. [printed p. 26]
- The masters of the land and the celestial gods generate an uninterrupted royal lineage. [printed p. 26]
- Imperial authority is connected directly to cosmogony from its beginning through the present, so legitimacy rests in continuity with creation rather than descent from a later founder. [printed p. 26]
- Detienne calls this “continuous creation,” but continuity is vulnerable to entropy. [printed p. 26]
- Periodic restoration therefore reconnects the world with living forces beyond it. Temples are rebuilt, domestic sanctuaries remade, and boundaries redefined at intervals. [printed p. 26]
- These repeated acts are regeneration rather than refoundation because the configuration posits no first foundation to repeat. [printed p. 26]
- Vedic India supplies a different refusal of foundation, one connected to resistance against localization and site-making. [printed p. 26]
- Detienne notes that Vedic communities inhabited villages, towns, and fortresses while valuing nomadism and expressing revulsion toward fixed localization. The contrast is between material sedentariness and the represented value of placeless mobility. [printed p. 26]
- The Vedic spatial configuration is open and lacks individualized temples or unique sacred sites. One space is treated as available everywhere. [printed pp. 26–27]
- A sacrificial precinct appears around fires lit by mobile sacrificers and disappears when they move. It is an intense but temporary module rather than a permanently founded site. [printed p. 27]
- Detienne calls that sacrificial configuration a complete nonplace. [printed p. 27]
- Comparison with Japan and Vedic India reveals that familiar “foundation” normally bundles together spatial singularity, temporal singularity, ritual action, and a personal founder. [printed p. 27]
- The spatial component is a uniquely named and characterized place whose boundary distinguishes it from a wider space. [printed p. 27]
- The temporal component is a recognized initial event located in history or chronology and treated as solemn, remarkable, or otherwise set apart. [printed p. 27]
- The process component is a significant beginning expected to initiate a historical sequence. [printed p. 27]
- The agentive component is an action, gesture, rite, or ceremony attached to a named person such as Romulus or Clovis who links a community to one unique place. [printed p. 27]
- The apparent transparency of foundation dissolves into a conceptual field of separable components. [printed p. 27]
- Across societies that all establish territories, some employ a strong idea of foundation, some invoke it only minimally, and others organize territorialization without it. [printed p. 27]

## V. Coining new comparables: place, boundaries, origins, founders, and objects

- The singular-plural comparativist carries a compact set of generative questions across communities whose differences increase as familiar bridges between them weaken. [printed p. 27]
- Detienne declines to establish a fixed outer limit for the possible field of comparison. [printed p. 27]
- Daoist China shows a configuration in which a holy place rather than a town is founded and the relevant founding gestures purify an altar and sacrificial precinct. [printed p. 27]
- Walled-city traditions from the Cameroon region unsettle an inherited Roman image in which a founder’s authority and a city’s fortifications are sustained by the blood of neighbouring people. [printed pp. 27–28]
- The number of cultures required for comparison is not determined by a demand for exhaustiveness. [printed p. 28]
- The objective is neither a universal abstract model of foundation nor a fine-grained classification of every cultural form of territorial creation. [printed p. 28]
- The objective is to refashion the analytic notion of foundation through repeated questions about what makes a place, what constitutes a boundary, and what beginning entails. [printed p. 28]
- The investigation seeks multiple and changing forms of place rather than an essential property shared by all places. [printed p. 28]
- Detienne inventories radically different representations of places: places may address or signal to people, receive impregnation, demand nourishment, or weep. [printed p. 28]
- Questions about place include whether it bears a name, whether it is fixed, what inhabiting it means, and whether residence necessarily entails alteration and construction. [printed p. 28]
- A place may be declared vacant, ritually purged, and available for seizure. Such declarations belong to territorialization rather than neutrally describing empty land. [printed p. 28]
- Chinese and African practices of geomancy exemplify learned systems or sciences of places and sites. [printed p. 28]
- A Greek philosophical configuration from an Attic village turned Academy makes place dependent on the moving body. Borders arise and disappear successively as the body moves. [printed p. 28]
- In that configuration, place trails behind movement as a trace or wake rather than remaining a fixed container. [printed p. 28]
- A mobile account of place blurs the apparently obvious image of a permanently fixed, geometrically bounded altar. [printed p. 28]
- Detienne’s boundary questions begin with genesis: does a boundary come from bush, village, the surface of the earth, or the earth’s unarticulated presence? [printed p. 28]
- A boundary may be fixed, shifting, porous, inviolable, or passable without obstruction. These are independent possibilities to investigate rather than synonyms for a single line. [printed p. 28]
- A boundary need not be visibly marked, inspected, or circumscribed. When it is marked, comparison must ask which material and ritual techniques made it legible. [printed p. 28]
- Boundary analysis also requires identifying who or what enforces observance. [printed p. 28]
- Boundaries may take the form of windows, hinges, or bolts, and may occupy a centre or hearth instead of an outer edge. [printed p. 28]
- Each further case of incomparability should provoke new conceptual language by exposing the strangeness concealed within familiar beginnings and founding gestures. [printed pp. 28–29]
- The inquiry expands from territorial foundation into different ways communities represent making, producing, creating, procreating, and inventing. [printed p. 29]
- It distinguishes origin from becoming and beginning, and asks how cosmogony coexists with starting, founding, and inaugurating. [printed p. 29]
- Across the Upper Volta, contemporary India, Rome, and early Greek cities, territorialization may involve literal or metaphorical cutting, encircling, laying a base, rooting, or setting down an infant. [printed p. 29]
- Some objects territorialize more readily than others. Detienne lists holy places, old houses, constructed altars, sacrificial precincts, and temples. [printed p. 29]
- Some constructions function as matrices for larger political-spatial wholes. In one Indian configuration, a temple can contain or instantiate an entire kingdom and inhabited land. [printed p. 29]
- Different traditions locate the source of founding in heaven, in earth, or in the meeting of something locally present with an arrival from elsewhere. [printed p. 29]
- Detienne compares Chinese, Roman, and Greek colonies as distinct configurations in which elements from different places are fused. [printed p. 29]
- Founder-carried objects may transport ancestors, sacrificial capacities, food, fire, or a common hearth into the new territory. [printed p. 29]
- Comparison must ask what foundation does with lineages, ancestors, and the dead. [printed p. 29]
- The possibility that ancestrality can be produced locally, after arrival, makes ancestral legitimacy an object of construction and has immediate implications for nationalist origin stories. [printed p. 29]
- Founders must be studied as figures wearing roles or masks within configurations, rather than treated as equivalent heroic individuals. [printed p. 29]
- In Mesopotamia, divine initiative precedes a king’s founding of towns and temples according to a plan established above. [printed p. 29]
- Elsewhere a mortal founder may be a hunter led by prey, a Hindu renouncer entering a forest, or an exiled murderer following a Delphic oracle. [printed p. 29]
- A founder’s initiative varies with the configuration of earth, local power, and the founder’s represented relation to them. [printed pp. 29–30]
- Where Burkina Faso’s Masters of the Earth hold effective power, a founder cannot act like the Greek *archegetes* who conquers supposedly empty land, marks it, and assigns its boundaries. [printed p. 30]
- Other founders proceed cautiously, creating an initial dwelling that already contains a village, a community, and ancestors. [printed p. 30]
- Some founders are moved by doubles, violence, or bloodshed that attaches them to the place. [printed p. 30]
- A foundation can absorb its founder, converting that figure into a special ancestor, an ordinary ancestor, a lineage-free dead figure, or an abstract political symbol. [printed p. 30]

## VI. From themes to mechanisms, microconfigurations, and “plates of coherence”

- Moving through foundation cases reveals a field of possibilities whose conceptual manipulation separates distinctive elements and shows their different arrangements. [printed p. 30]
- Detienne redescribes the broad founding category as the creation of territory across roughly a dozen selected cultures. [printed p. 30]
- The principal analytic operation is logical dismantling: identify two or three elements, observe their interaction, and isolate microconfigurations in which differences become increasingly and subtly interrelated. [printed p. 30]
- The method studies mechanisms of thought through patterns that become visible from several unobtrusive, non-thematic angles. [printed p. 30]
- Productive entry points include an inaugural figure from outside, a local Earth-force, and nonbeginning together with what follows from it. [printed p. 30]
- Applying one entry point across two, three, or four cultures produces schemata that can be similar without becoming identical. [printed p. 30]
- “Nonbeginning” can connect Vedic India, Japan, and the Guayaki and Yanomami of South America while preserving their distinct arrangements. [printed pp. 30–31]
- In Vedic India, nonbeginning is joined to nonplace and rejection of a singular time. [printed p. 31]
- In Japan, cosmogony sustains nonbeginning while requiring continuous regeneration and restoration. [printed p. 31]
- In the South American forest cases, nonbeginning is joined to refusal of permanent sites, movement within one territory, transport of a temporary centre, erasure of that centre, and erasure of traces left by the dead. [printed p. 31]
- Changing the entry point to remains or the dead as anchors permits a different comparison among Vedic India, the Guayaki, and Greek *archegetes*. [printed p. 31]
- The same cases can therefore participate in more than one constructed comparison depending on the relation being investigated. [printed p. 31]
- The local-Earth-force entry point connects configurations in Burkina Faso and Daoist China without claiming that their Earth figures are equivalent. [printed p. 31]
- In Kasena territory in Burkina Faso, primeval Earth incorporates already-present troglodytes and pieces of bush understood as the earth’s skin, beneath which no stable foundation exists. [printed p. 31]
- A Master of the Earth controls attempts at territorialization by the wandering Filani Stranger. [printed p. 31]
- In Daoist China, a Ground god inhabits every holy place and may also appear as a Wall god. [printed p. 31]
- In royal-foundation traditions, the Ground god is doubled between a son of Sky represented as a sword dancer and a son of the First Rebel represented as a victim buried under a doorway to anchor the precinct wall. [printed p. 31]
- This doubled Ground creates a holy-place foundation that maintains relations to both Sky and Earth. [printed p. 31]
- The comparable units are mechanisms observable in relations among elements arranged by a chosen entry point, not free-floating themes such as “founder,” “earth,” or “boundary.” [printed p. 31]
- Borrowing Gérard Lenclud’s formulation, Detienne describes these mechanisms as localized sequences of quasi-causal connection. [printed p. 31]
- Once a significant feature or attitude, such as selection or rejection, is detected, it must be situated in a configuration. Its relation to the rest of that configuration is hypothesized to be nonrandom. [printed pp. 31–32]
- Detienne consequently postulates relative rather than total coherence, expressed as localized “plates of coherence.” [printed p. 32]
- The methodological hypothesis is that the elements of a microconfiguration have an interrelation and collectively display an orientation. [printed p. 32]
- Comparables can be formulated as orientations composed of linked selections made against other available possibilities. [printed p. 32]
- Detienne explicitly qualifies the choosing society as local rather than global and as committed only to the microconfiguration under analysis. [printed p. 32]
- The singular-plural analyst seeks the constraints shaping each configuration. [printed p. 32]
- A microsystem organizes its central elements in relation to an angle such as nonbeginning or a local Earth-force. [printed p. 32]
- Detienne rejects typologies of founder kinds, such as hunter versus wanderer or cautious versus impetuous founder. [printed p. 32]
- He also rejects a morphology that would classify the forms of territories or households. [printed p. 32]
- The relevant comparables are interconnecting plates organized by an initial selection, and the object of interest is the grouping generated by a logical solution. [printed p. 32]
- Such groupings may resemble one another while remaining different in their specific interconnections. [printed p. 32]

## VII. Greek microcomparison: external founders, autochthony, and refoundation

- Detienne turns to comparison within Greek communities to show that constructive comparison need not always pair Greece with China or India. [printed pp. 32–33]
- Twelve centuries of Greek history and roughly seven hundred cities offer numerous observable microconfigurations within a conventionally unified civilizational field. [printed p. 33]
- He initially distinguishes two founding orientations: the external *archegetes* and autochthony. [printed p. 33]
- In many small archaic cities, the *archegetes* is an inaugural figure arriving from elsewhere. [printed p. 33]
- Detienne’s *Apollo, the Butcher* associates this figure with paths, firmly planted feet, thresholds, doors, and precincts cut out by a knife. [printed p. 33]
- The *archegetes* declares land empty and deserted, cuts out a section, and circumscribes the site of a first altar. [printed p. 33]
- The founder sacrifices an animal at that altar and distributes portions of equal weight among members of the new community. Founding links violent division of space to a political distribution among citizens. [printed p. 33]
- This external founder establishes a city rather than merely a house or temple. [printed p. 33]
- The model appears to recognize neither an already-present population nor a local Earth-power in the seized territory. [printed p. 33]
- Hesiod’s Gaia supplies a contrasting Earth figure who founds herself and is accordingly self-founded. [printed p. 33]
- The sanctuary from which the *archegetes* departs links the mobile outside actor to an enduring power, Themis at Delphi, whose oracle creates verbal paths toward making and establishment. [printed p. 33]
- The same configuration relates Poseidon as the power of bases to Hestia as hearth, centre, sacrificial fire, and primordial purpose. [printed p. 33]
- Autochthony contrasts with an outside founder acting while Earth remains silent. [printed pp. 33–34]
- Athenians aggressively asserted autochthony against other Greeks, and that configuration became dominant during the fifth century BCE conventionally celebrated as a Golden Age. [printed p. 34]
- Detienne mocks “armchair” periodization and academicians who treat fifth-century Athens as their intellectual preserve. His polemic connects scholarly ownership of a canon to uncritical reverence for the period. [printed p. 34]
- Athenians represented themselves as direct children of Attica’s timeless Earth and distinguished their pedigree from ordinary earthborn peoples elsewhere. [printed p. 34]
- Detienne reads Isocrates, Plato in pastiche, and Euripides ironically as articulating or exposing a purity discourse directed against *perioikoi* and resident foreigners. [printed p. 34]
- The presence of residents without the authorized pedigree becomes an imagined contamination and an insult to Erechtheus, Athena’s Earth-born child. [printed p. 34]
- Attica is essentialized as a pure place in a manner Detienne compares to later essentialization of “the Past.” [printed p. 34]
- Pure Athenian identity becomes a claim to pure Greek identity. [printed p. 34]
- The citizenship law of 451 BCE required Athenian descent through both paternal and maternal lines, although Detienne notes that the size of its legislative majority is unknown. [printed p. 34]
- His sarcastic celebration of the maternal requirement as feminist exposes the mistake of treating women’s use in pedigree control as women’s emancipation. [printed p. 34]
- Detienne asks why Periclean legislation was needed if authentic autochthonous identity had truly been continuous and self-evident. The legal act reveals active production of the identity it purports to recognize. [printed p. 34]
- The Athenian configuration supplies an oblique comparison with neighbouring Thebes and shows that nearby comparison can be as conceptually disruptive as distant comparison. [printed p. 34]
- Detienne deliberately turns to Thebes before Oedipus and before the later interpretive industry centred on Oedipus. [printed p. 34]
- Cadmus is another external inaugural figure who follows Apollo’s oracle and a conspicuously dappled heifer to the place where her collapse, sacrifice, and the founding of a city should occur. [printed pp. 34–35]
- The future Theban site differs from land declared empty because something is already there: a serpent born from Ares and the vengeful Erinys Tilphussa. [printed p. 35]
- Ares, also represented as Palaichthon or Old Man of the Earth, and Earth itself belong to the already-present autochthonous cluster. [printed p. 35]
- Cadmus kills the spring-guarding serpent while seeking water for the founding sacrifice and thereby incurs Ares’ anger. [printed p. 35]
- To complete the Delphically ordered foundation, Cadmus must sow the dragon’s teeth in the earth. [printed p. 35]
- Armed earthborn Spartoi emerge and immediately kill one another until five remain as the first Theban citizens. The founding population is generated through autochthony and intragroup slaughter after an outsider’s violence against a local guardian. [printed p. 35]
- Detienne treats the Theban material as several interconnected yet divergently oriented relations that call for analysis through refoundation. [printed p. 35]
- He abruptly says that this analysis is impossible in Thebes because Thebes is “off-limits,” then shifts to recurring refoundation configurations in other cities. The reason for the prohibition is not supplied in this chapter. [printed p. 35]
- Athens becomes the preferred case precisely because its configuration is anomalous enough to have been dismissed by specialists in autochthony. [printed p. 35]
- Detienne acknowledges discontinuities and discrepancies among Erechtheus, snake-tailed Cecrops, and Erichthonius, whose conception joins Hephaestus’ semen, Athena’s evasion, and Earth. [printed p. 35]
- He brackets those genealogical discrepancies as secondary and makes Poseidon’s violent return the principal relation in the refoundation sequence. [printed p. 35]
- Poseidon, allied with Eumolpus of Eleusis and Thracian cavalry, wages war against Athena’s side. [printed pp. 35–36]
- An oracle makes the city’s survival depend on the blood of Erechtheus, beginning with the sacrifice of one of his daughters to Persephone or parched Earth. [printed p. 36]
- Erechtheus is then driven into the Acropolis earth and merged with Poseidon under the compound identity Poseidon-Erechtheus. [printed p. 36]
- The sacrificed daughter, buried Erechtheus, and victorious Poseidon become the restored basis of Athens. [printed p. 36]
- The refoundation secures and deepens an autochthony that had been represented as primordial. Apparent continuity is maintained through violent restorative change. [printed p. 36]
- Solon supplies a political-juridical variant by describing the city as founded upon *thesmoi*, durable words or laws intended to stand for approximately a century. [printed p. 36]
- Solon’s formulation recognizes repeated foundation: he is neither the first nor the last founder. [printed p. 36]
- Detienne argues that constructed comparison can clear away habits of Athenian historiography by reconnecting fifth-century events with the imaginary configurations through which Athens represented itself. [printed p. 36]

## VIII. What comparison is for, and the objections Detienne answers

- The question of comparison’s utility repeatedly arises in elite universities and is voiced, in Detienne’s polemical portrait, by classicists. [printed p. 36]
- He presents the question as an institutionalized demand that anthropology justify itself before classical history rather than as neutral methodological curiosity. [printed pp. 36–37]
- Braudel’s *The Identity of France* provides one answer: retrospective France, rich in past experiences, becomes ideal terrain for comparative history. [printed pp. 36–37]
- Detienne quotes Braudel’s further definition of comparative history as a search for similarities and a necessary condition of social science. [printed p. 37]
- Detienne finds that similarity-first conception inadequate because it preserves a nationally bounded object and restricts comparison to recognizable resemblance. [printed p. 37]
- Moses Finley’s question asks how anthropology is useful to ancient history. Detienne reads the formulation as making one discipline a service provider to another. [printed p. 37]
- Finley’s portrayal of anthropology assigns it the task of creating general laws, which Detienne says would flatten differences. [printed p. 37]
- The predictable historical objection then charges anthropology with neglecting context and repeats the rule that only already-comparable objects should be compared. [printed p. 37]
- Detienne treats these recurring objections as durable prejudices, although he does not reconstruct their strongest methodological form in this chapter. [printed p. 37]
- His direct answer is a chosen commitment to history open to all human societies across time and space. [printed p. 37]
- He opposes that horizon to parochial history and to national history organized around France as an exclusive territory. [printed p. 37]
- Anthropology leads this expanded project because it carries a strong comparative orientation across societies. [printed p. 37]
- The objective is not to impose general laws explaining all variables and constants in human cultural invention. [printed p. 37]
- Historians and anthropologists should instead construct comparables and analyse microsystems of thought organized by an initial selection. [printed p. 37]
- The selected microconfiguration can be set beside a different selection made in a society with no historical knowledge of the first. Genealogical transmission is therefore unnecessary for analytic comparison. [printed p. 37]
- Detienne answers the charge that comparison inevitably collapses into naive analogy. [printed pp. 37–38]
- His rejected procedure begins with a completed, well-defined object from the familiar side, crosses a national or cultural frontier, finds something that resembles it, and annexes the second object to the first. [printed p. 38]
- Constructing comparables avoids that transfer because comparison changes the initial object and its terms rather than transporting it intact. [printed p. 38]
- Detienne also distinguishes his method from typology and morphology, both of which stabilize recurrent forms rather than reconstructing relations within microconfigurations. [printed p. 38]
- A remaining objector may still ask what practical use follows from detecting mechanisms of thought. [printed p. 38]
- Detienne replies that the same demand could be made of any explanation that identifies causality or connected relations within cultural, economic, or social systems. [printed p. 38]
- Intellectual understanding of people elsewhere or in the past is itself a legitimate priority and should not require defence before an imagined tribunal of immediate utility. [printed p. 38]
- Nationalism is a major obstacle because it produces a dense national object and helps keep history separated from ethnology. [printed p. 38]
- Nationalist historians may ceremonially honour Marc Bloch, whom Detienne presents as a resistance martyr and a martyr of comparative history, while refusing Bloch’s comparative adventure. [printed p. 38]
- The nation becomes holy and incomparable, and its scholarly guardians are authorized by academic qualifications and by a mission transmitted through teachers. [printed p. 38]
- Comparison has an ethical virtue because it places the values and selections of one’s own society in perspective. [printed pp. 38–39]
- Detienne applies this decentring task to societies into which one is born, societies one chooses, and societies in which one becomes a resident through personal history, with varying degrees of assimilation, acceptance, and acculturation. [printed pp. 38–39]
- Constructing more or less robust comparables in a collaborative group can create distance from inherited impulses and bring one’s own traditions under critical scrutiny. [printed p. 39]
- The resulting insight is that one’s tradition is probably one selection among others rather than the inevitable arrangement of human life. [printed p. 39]
- People remain free, at their own risk, to deepen an attachment to a landscape or nation after comparison has placed that attachment in perspective. [printed p. 39]
- The demanding comparative aim is to understand multiple cultures as well as they understood themselves, relate them to one another, and recognize the differences their configurations construct. [printed p. 39]
- Detienne proposes that this relational understanding teaches people to live with others. [printed p. 39]
- Following Tzvetan Todorov, he names detachment from oneself and genuine understanding of social factors as a practical end of comparison. [printed p. 39]

## IX. Condensed reconstruction of Detienne’s comparison procedure

- **1. Assemble plural expertise.** Form a small, recurring group whose members retain deep field competence while learning one another’s questions and materials. [printed p. 24]
- **2. Choose a middle-strength probe.** Begin with a category broad enough to reach several cases but weak enough to be altered by them. [printed p. 25]
- **3. Permit distant and nearby cases.** Do not require common ancestry, adjacency, equal “development,” or visible similarity before comparison. [printed pp. 23–24]
- **4. Carry the same questions, not a finished object.** Move a compact question set among cases rather than exporting one culture’s completed definition. [printed pp. 27–28, 38]
- **5. Welcome refusal and absence.** Treat a case lacking the presumed feature as the event that reveals the category’s hidden components. [printed p. 26]
- **6. Dismantle the category.** Separate spatial, temporal, ritual, agentive, material, ancestral, and political relations that ordinary language had bundled together. [printed pp. 27, 29–31]
- **7. Select an entry angle.** Recompare cases through nonbeginning, the dead as anchors, Earth-power, outside figures, or another relation exposed in the dismantling. [printed pp. 30–31]
- **8. Construct microconfigurations.** Identify a few interacting elements and the constraints that make their local arrangement nonrandom. [printed pp. 30–32]
- **9. Compare orientations.** Set linked local selections beside other linked selections without converting them into identical types. [printed p. 32]
- **10. Revisit the starting object.** Use the contrast to discover overlooked features in the initially familiar case and to reconstruct its category. [printed pp. 23, 25–27]
- **11. Stop short of exhaustive taxonomy.** The method aims at conceptual precision and relational understanding rather than a complete catalogue or universal law. [printed pp. 28, 37]
- **12. Return comparison critically home.** Use the new comparables to expose the contingency of one’s own disciplinary, cultural, and national assumptions. [printed pp. 38–39]

## X. Academic, credentialist, institutional, and boundary notes already explicit in the chapter

### Academic and disciplinary institutions

- Research centres and laboratories make cross-disciplinary work structurally possible, yet Detienne argues that identity-defensive habits survive inside those institutions. Institutional proximity does not by itself remove disciplinary ownership. [printed pp. 22–23]
- “My field” and claims of a culture’s total originality act as intellectual boundary claims. They defend a specialist’s jurisdiction over an object before the comparative inquiry begins. [printed p. 23]
- Advice to compare only adjacent, similarly classified societies functions as an epistemic licensing system: comparison must first satisfy criteria established by guardians of method. [printed p. 23]
- The Prague example makes unauthorized experimentation epistemically productive. Detienne emphasizes that the linguists did not wait for epistemologists to issue permission. [printed p. 23]
- A “professional interpreter” contributes necessary depth, yet professional identity should be an origin point rather than an exclusive entitlement to interpret a civilization. [printed p. 24]
- Shared intellectual life creates distributed competence that cannot be reduced to individual credentials. Detienne values the functioning microcommunity and recurring meeting over the prestige signal of a large grant. [printed p. 24]
- The Japan specialists’ prolonged silence and embarrassment when their material did not fit “foundation” show a social pressure inside collaborative inquiry: specialists may initially experience nonfit as failure even though it is the method’s most valuable result. [printed p. 26]
- Academicians’ possessive relation to fifth-century Athens exemplifies canon ownership. Detienne connects scholarly period worship with failure to see the political construction of Golden-Age purity. [printed p. 34]
- The recurring question about anthropology’s utility to classics establishes an institutional hierarchy in which the externally positioned discipline must justify its contribution to the canon-owning discipline. [printed pp. 36–37]
- Finley’s question is staged in an elite Cambridge social setting and asks for collective approval. Detienne portrays collegial prestige rituals as helping reproduce a restricted view of legitimate comparison. [printed p. 37]
- Braudel’s status as a newly elected Academician sits beside a nostalgic, nationally contained comparative programme. Detienne uses the juxtaposition to associate intellectual prestige with preservation of France as the privileged historical object. [printed p. 37]
- The chorus defending contexts and pre-existing comparability exemplifies disciplinary gatekeeping through a methodological language that can sound neutral. [printed p. 37]
- Nationalist scholars can appropriate the prestige of Marc Bloch through memorial ritual while declining the comparative practice for which Detienne honours him. Commemoration can domesticate a thinker whose method would disrupt institutional boundaries. [printed p. 38]
- Academic qualifications explicitly appear as warrants authorizing guardians of the incomparable nation. Credentials validate a mission received from earlier teachers and reproduce national-historical custody across generations. [printed p. 38]
- Detienne’s critique is specifically directed at credentials as warrants for guardianship and exclusion. The chapter does not claim that qualifications, expertise, or teaching are inherently worthless. [printed pp. 24, 38]

### Boundaries as an analytic problem

- The Brahmin Indian example reverses a common causal picture: the village generates its boundary rather than a prior boundary generating the village. Boundaries can be effects of collective organization. [printed p. 25]
- Japanese periodic regeneration includes redefining boundaries. A boundary can persist through alteration rather than through an unchanged founding line. [printed p. 26]
- The familiar foundation bundle assumes a named unique place bounded inside a larger space. Japan and Vedic India reveal that this bounded spatial singularity is contingent. [printed pp. 26–27]
- Boundary inquiry must distinguish genesis, material form, location, permeability, durability, marking, inspection, enforcement, and relation to movement. [printed p. 28]
- A boundary may move with bodies and appear successively, so it cannot be presumed to be a static perimeter. [printed p. 28]
- A boundary may be central rather than peripheral, as in a hearth, doorway, window, hinge, or bolt. [printed p. 28]
- Declaring land vacant is a political-territorial operation that permits seizure. “Empty” is an authorized representation of a place, not merely a visual observation. [printed pp. 28, 30, 33]
- A conqueror marks and assigns boundaries because the configuration grants him authority over a space represented as empty. A Master-of-the-Earth configuration redistributes that authority to an already-present local force. [printed p. 30]
- The victim buried under a doorway in the Daoist royal configuration anchors a precinct wall. Boundary stability is represented as an effect of death, sacrifice, and a relation between Sky, rebellion, Ground, and royal order. [printed p. 31]
- The Greek *archegetes* joins paths, thresholds, doors, incision, altar, sacrifice, equal distribution, and city-making. The boundary is one relation in a political-sacrificial configuration. [printed p. 33]
- Athenian citizenship law converts mythical territorial autochthony into a genealogical membership boundary policed through both parents. Spatial origin and civic exclusion reinforce each other. [printed p. 34]
- The social boundary between pedigree citizens and residents is narrated as purity versus contamination. Mythic descent naturalizes a legal-political distinction. [printed p. 34]

## XI. Analytic tensions, objections, and validation owed

*These are critical deductions from the chapter. They are not presented as Detienne’s own concessions unless a bullet says so.*

- The claim that studying distant times and distant places is methodologically inseparable “on the whole” needs qualification. Archival survival, field access, language, participant response, and colonial relations produce different evidentiary conditions for historians and anthropologists. [inference from printed p. 22]
- Detienne expands the proper field to all human societies while proposing microgroups of two to four researchers. The method explains collaborative intensity more clearly than it explains coverage at that scale. [inference from printed pp. 23–24]
- His preference for regular meetings over large grants comes from one decade-long collaborative history and should be treated as a practice judgment, not a demonstrated general law of research organization. [inference from printed p. 24]
- The middle-strength category is central, but the chapter gives no operational test for when a category is too strong or too weak. Calibration remains dependent on collective judgment and the quality of specialist reactions. [inference from printed p. 25]
- Specialist discomfort may reveal a productive nonfit, yet it may also reflect hierarchy inside the group. The chapter does not explain how disagreement is recorded, how a minority specialist can block a forced comparison, or who decides that an absence has been established. [inference from printed p. 26]
- “According to the most ancient texts” limits the Japanese finding to a textual corpus. Absence of founding in surviving authoritative texts does not by itself establish absence from all historical Japanese practices, regions, genres, or periods. [inference from printed p. 26]
- The Vedic account similarly moves from selected representations to broad formulations about what “the Indians” valued or found revolting. Internal diversity, textual genre, and historical change require independent checking. [inference from printed pp. 26–27]
- Declining exhaustiveness leaves the stopping rule unresolved. A comparison may become conceptually productive after three cases, yet an unexamined fourth case might overturn the constructed orientation. [inference from printed p. 28]
- Detienne rejects essential qualities while repeatedly writing that a culture or society thinks, chooses, rejects, or orients itself. Even with his local-microconfiguration qualification, this language can reify internally contested representations as collective mental acts. [inference from printed pp. 29, 31–32]
- The term “mechanisms of thought” may suggest stable causal machinery where the evidence establishes only patterned relations among selected representations. [inference from printed pp. 30–31]
- Relative coherence is explicitly a hypothesis that element distribution is nonrandom. If treated as a required result, it could make analysts force disorder, contradiction, accident, or multivocality into a coherent orientation. [inference from printed pp. 31–32]
- “Quasi-causal” connection remains underspecified. The chapter does not state what evidence distinguishes causation, constraint, analogy, narrative association, and the analyst’s retrospective construction. [inference from printed p. 31]
- An “initial choice” may be an analytic reconstruction rather than a historically conscious choice. The vocabulary risks attributing unified agency and a decision point to processes that may be distributed, cumulative, coercive, or unconscious. [inference from printed pp. 32, 37]
- Detienne opposes typology, yet recurring axes such as outside/local, beginning/nonbeginning, place/nonplace, fixed/mobile, and foundation/regeneration can harden into an implicit typology if detached from each local relation set. [inference from printed pp. 26–32]
- Comparison through a common question avoids transferring a completed object, but the question itself still comes from somewhere. The method needs a reflexive record of who coined the entry point, in which language, and which cases it initially privileged. [inference from printed pp. 25, 27–31]
- Condensed movement across Japan, Vedic India, Burkina Faso, Daoist China, South American peoples, Cameroon, Mesopotamia, and Greece can reproduce decontextualization unless each microconfiguration is independently supported by specialist evidence. [inference from printed pp. 26–33]
- The chapter sometimes moves between myths, rituals, legal rules, built forms, political institutions, and scholarly reconstructions as elements of one configuration. Their evidentiary status and historical relation should be distinguished rather than presumed. [inference from printed pp. 26–36]
- The external-founder model declares land empty, while Detienne critically treats emptiness as a representation. The analysis should also identify the people whose presence the source or founder erases, rather than staying solely within the founding culture’s categories. [inference from printed pp. 28, 30, 33]
- The Theban case is declared “off-limits” at the moment its divergent relations become most difficult. Because no reason is supplied, the shift to Athens looks like an unresolved analytic withdrawal. [inference from printed p. 35]
- In Athens, Detienne dismisses genealogical discontinuities as trifles to foreground Poseidon. That decision may reproduce the very selective coherence-building his method needs to guard against. [inference from printed p. 35]
- The Athenian sequence shows relations among autochthony, legal descent, sacrifice, war, and refoundation, but the chapter does not fully establish causal direction among them. [inference from printed pp. 34–36]
- Detienne’s sarcasm toward classicists, academicians, nationalists, and Finley identifies real boundary-making claims but does not present the strongest contextualist objection. The polemic can make a serious problem of evidentiary comparability look like mere cowardice or prejudice. [inference from printed pp. 34, 36–38]
- Credentialed guardianship is sharply criticized, while the method simultaneously depends on professional interpreters with deep language and field competence. The chapter supports opposition to proprietary credentials, but it does not support replacing expertise with unrestricted analogy. [inference from printed pp. 24, 38]
- Comparative detachment is an ethical aspiration rather than an automatic effect. Comparison has also historically served colonial classification and hierarchy, a danger this chapter does not address directly. [inference from printed pp. 38–39]
- Understanding cultures “as well as they understood themselves” presupposes that a culture has an adequately recoverable and internally coherent self-understanding. Multiple classes, genders, factions, regions, genres, and periods make that standard difficult to define. [inference from printed p. 39]
- Seeing one’s tradition as one selection among others produces critical distance, but it does not establish that all selections are ethically equivalent or equally voluntary. [inference from printed p. 39]
- The chapter’s ethical conclusion does not specify how comparative knowledge moves from scholarly microgroups into social relations or whether it reliably improves coexistence. [inference from printed p. 39]

## XII. Provisional Polymyth applications

*These are unratified project inferences. They identify possible uses; they do not amend Polymyth, CORE, CORE+, ML*, or any coherence specification.*

### Comparative method and corpus design

- A Polymyth comparison can begin with a deliberately provisional feature cluster whose function is to generate questions. A resistant work should trigger revision of the feature rather than automatic failure of the work. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 25–26]
- Category strength can be treated as a design constraint: a field that decides the result before reading is too strong, while a field that produces no discriminating question is too weak. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 25]
- Corpus construction should permit distant works with no genealogy or direct influence between them when they instantiate comparable relational problems. Historical influence and analytic comparison should remain separate fields. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 23–24, 37]
- A completed category from one source tradition should not be exported intact to another. Polymyth can record the starting term, the case that fractured it, and the revised components produced by the comparison. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 26–27, 38]
- Negative evidence deserves an explicit role. “No founder,” “no unique site,” “no singular beginning,” and “no durable boundary” can be productive configurations rather than missing-data defects. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 26–27]
- Analysis should compare works through small relational configurations rather than whole-culture labels or broad themes. “Founding” alone is weak; founder origin, local Earth-power, boundary act, sacrifice, citizenship distribution, and treatment of prior inhabitants form a testable configuration. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 30–33]
- The same work can enter multiple comparisons through different entry angles, as nonbeginning cases are regrouped through dead remains or ephemeral centres. Corpus membership should therefore be many-to-many rather than forcing each work into one definitive type. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 31]
- Salience can be discovered contrastively: a feature becomes salient because another work reveals that the feature could have been arranged differently. It should not always be preassigned by theme frequency. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 23, 31–32]
- Nonexhaustive analysis can remain legitimate if it is explicitly provisional, preserves the cases tested, and can be reopened when a new work fractures the current comparable. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 26, 28]
- Every constructed comparable should preserve its entry question, selected elements, source evidence, dissent, and stopping point. This would make the analyst’s construction auditable instead of presenting an orientation as an objective property waiting in the text. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 24–25, 30–32]

### Coherence analysis

- Detienne’s “plates of coherence” offers a close analogue for local narrative coherence: limited groups of elements may form constrained relations without requiring a whole mythology, culture, or work to possess one total orientation. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 31–32]
- A coherence judgment should distinguish a source-supported relation from a merely repeated motif. Similar snakes, founders, or sacrifices do not establish comparable logic until their relations to place, authority, sequence, and community are reconstructed. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 31–32, 35]
- Relative coherence should remain a falsifiable hypothesis. Contradictory evidence must be allowed to show that a proposed plate is incomplete, contested, layered, or absent. [Polymyth inference and caution from printed pp. 31–32]
- “Initial choice” can be operationalized as an analytic orientation rather than a claim that an entire society consciously decided once. This preserves Detienne’s contrast while avoiding false collective agency. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 32, 37]
- Microconfigurations can distinguish coherent transformation from simple inconsistency. Japanese continuity includes periodic alteration because regeneration is structurally required by entropy; the change belongs to the orientation rather than violating it. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 26]
- Refoundation can likewise explain how a myth preserves a claim of primordial continuity through a later violent re-grounding. Continuity and rupture can be linked rather than scored as an immediate contradiction. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 35–36]
- The Athenian genealogical discrepancies warn against discarding inconvenient details to protect a preferred orientation. A Polymyth audit should record what the analyst bracketed and whether the conclusion survives restoring it. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 35]
- A text’s apparent “absence” should be scoped to the evidence examined. “No foundation in these oldest texts” is defensible; “this culture has no foundation” requires much broader validation. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 26]

### Foundation, origin, and territorialization lenses

- A reusable Polymyth foundation matrix could separately track: unique versus repeatable space; named versus unnamed place; fixed versus mobile site; singular beginning versus nonbeginning; founder versus distributed agency; outside arrival versus local emergence; founding versus regeneration or refoundation; and city, house, temple, kingdom, or precinct as the object established. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 26–33]
- Other useful foundation fields are: what is declared already present; what is declared empty; who authorizes that declaration; which gestures create the place; what violence or sacrifice occurs; what is distributed; how the dead or ancestors are used; what objects travel; and what enforces the result. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 28–33]
- Origin, beginning, becoming, creation, procreation, invention, founding, inauguration, restoration, and regeneration should remain distinct terms unless a source explicitly joins them. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 26, 29]
- A founder is better modelled as a position in a relation set than as a universal character type. The same apparent hero changes meaning depending on Earth-power, divine plan, oracle, violence, ancestors, and subsequent absorption into the foundation. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 29–32]
- Imported objects such as ancestral remains, fire, utensils, or sacrificial tools can be treated as mechanisms that transport continuity into a new territory rather than as decorative motifs. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 29]
- Claims of empty land should be flagged as mythic-political operations with possible erased prior inhabitants. This is important for comparing colonial and national foundation narratives. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 28–30, 33]
- Autochthony should be examined as a relation among place, descent, purity, citizenship, outsiders, and law, rather than merely tagged as “born from Earth.” [Polymyth inference from printed p. 34]
- A refoundation lens can identify narratives in which a supposedly timeless order requires later sacrifice, merger, law, or war to become stable. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 35–36]

### Boundary analysis

- Polymyth should avoid treating “boundary” as a single scalar. At minimum it can distinguish origin, location, visibility, material form, mobility, permeability, durability, ritual status, enforcement, and who is included or excluded. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 28]
- Boundaries can be generated by a community, generate a community, or emerge reciprocally with it. The causal direction should be recorded rather than assumed. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 25]
- A boundary may lie at an edge, doorway, window, hearth, or centre, and it may move with bodies. Spatial diagrams that assume a static outer perimeter could therefore misrepresent the mythic logic. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 28]
- Periodic boundary redefinition can express continuity, as in Japanese regeneration, rather than boundary failure. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 26]
- Boundary enforcement may be divine, ritual, architectural, genealogical, legal, military, ancestral, or scholarly. These enforcement modes should not be collapsed into a generic prohibition. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 28, 31, 33–34]
- Interpersonal “boundary” discourse should not be imported directly from this chapter. Detienne is analysing territorial, ritual, civic, bodily-spatial, and disciplinary boundaries; any extension to personal relationships would require a separately argued comparable. [Polymyth inference and scope guard from printed pp. 25–34, 36–38]

### Academia, credentialism, and interpretive authority

- Polymyth can distinguish expertise from proprietary jurisdiction. Deep specialist knowledge supplies evidence, while the specialist’s claim that outsiders cannot compare the material is an additional authority claim requiring justification. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 23–24]
- Credentials can be tracked as institutional authorization mechanisms when they certify guardianship over a nation, canon, period, or disciplinary object. Detienne’s target is the political use of qualification, not learning itself. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 24, 34, 38]
- A canon can be “territorialized” by scholars who claim a period as their preserve, define legitimate entry, and police which external questions count as useful. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 34, 36–37]
- Methodological phrases such as context, specificity, originality, and comparability can express valid evidentiary concerns or function as jurisdictional barriers. Their concrete use must be tested rather than automatically accepted or dismissed. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 22–23, 37]
- The Japanese specialists’ silence suggests a useful audit question for collaborative Polymyth work: did the process make a contributor report nonfit early, and did it preserve that nonfit as evidence rather than pressure the contributor to repair the category? [Polymyth inference from printed p. 26]
- Collaborative competence can be treated as a documented network property. Authorship, field expertise, questions supplied by others, points of dissent, and category revisions should remain visible. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 24]
- Detienne’s critique of utility tests can support analysis of how one discipline subordinates another by demanding immediate service to a pre-existing object. It does not exempt comparative claims from evidentiary validation. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 36–38]
- Memorializing a critical scholar while neutralizing the scholar’s disruptive method is a reusable institutional pattern, but each proposed instance requires evidence of both ceremonial appropriation and methodological refusal. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 38]

### Ethical and political use

- Comparison can return a tradition’s values to view as contingent selections, which provides a structural route for criticizing nationalist inevitability and civilizational exceptionalism. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 38–39]
- Purity narratives can be examined for how mythical descent, territorial essence, legal membership, and resident exclusion reinforce one another. [Polymyth inference from printed p. 34]
- Comparative decentring should be an aim and an audit question, not presumed as an automatic moral achievement. A comparison may still reproduce hierarchy, extractive classification, or credentialed dominance. [Polymyth inference and caution from printed pp. 38–39]
- The strongest Polymyth use of this chapter is procedural: an apparent incomparable should deepen the question, fracture the category, and reveal hidden relations in every case, including the case that originally supplied the category. [Polymyth inference from printed pp. 25–32]

## XIII. Chapter-level synthesis

- Detienne replaces comparison between ready-made, visibly similar objects with the collaborative construction of limited relational comparables. The construction begins from a provisional category, welcomes a resistant case, dismantles the category into interacting elements, and compares locally constrained orientations without seeking a universal typology. [synthesis of printed pp. 22–38]
- The chapter’s central evidentiary promise is reciprocal revision: Japan, Vedic India, Burkina Faso, Daoist China, South American cases, and Greek communities do not merely receive the category of foundation; their resistance and difference alter what foundation, place, boundary, beginning, founder, and coherence can mean. [synthesis of printed pp. 25–36]
- Its central institutional argument is that national, disciplinary, and credentialed guardianship often protects an object by declaring it unique, context-bound, or useful only within an established field. Collaborative comparison challenges that custody while continuing to depend on deep specialist knowledge. [synthesis of printed pp. 22–24, 34, 36–38]
- Its central unresolved methodological risk is that the analyst may manufacture the nonrandom orientation the method expects. The protections implied by the chapter are local scope, plural expertise, resistance from incomparable cases, and refusal of total typology; explicit dissent, falsification, and stopping procedures remain to be supplied. [critical synthesis from printed pp. 24–32]
