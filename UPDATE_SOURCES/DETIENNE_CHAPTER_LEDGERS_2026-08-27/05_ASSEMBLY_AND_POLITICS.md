## Chapter-level argument

- **SC:** Democracy and political invention should be studied as multiple beginnings rather than as the unique export of Periclean Athens or any other privileged origin. Similar assembly practices arose in societies separated by time, geography, and historical contact. (pp. 79-81; PDF pp. 94-96)
- **SC:** Comparison should begin with concrete practices: who may convene, enter, speak, propose, deliberate, decide, record, publicize, enforce, and represent the collectivity; how people position themselves; and what spatial and ritual forms make those acts possible. (pp. 81-82; PDF pp. 96-97)
- **SC:** These practices do more than express an already constituted political community. They progressively give the collectivity a form, define its common affairs, generate a public space, and cultivate a representation of its sovereignty over itself. (pp. 82-83, 88-96; PDF pp. 97-98, 103-111)
- **SC:** Assembly geometry, speech ritual, property distribution, military organization, publicity, writing, law, voting, and civic ideals are mutually formative components of distinct political microconfigurations. (pp. 84-98; PDF pp. 99-113)
- **SC:** Equality is neither a universal starting point nor a single condition. Resemblance, equal shares, equal speech, equality before law, consent, interchangeability, majority rule, consensus, and the cultivation of civic virtue are historically distinct constructions. (pp. 96-98; PDF pp. 111-113)
- **SC:** Comparison is valuable because adjacent configurations make one another intelligible through their differential features. The goal is to expose a field of possible political forms and identify how each microconfiguration diverges from others, rather than to force all cases into one institutional genealogy. (pp. 98-99; PDF pp. 113-114)

## Page-by-page exhaustive notes

### Printed p. 79 / PDF p. 94

- **SC:** Detienne opens by attacking the familiar story that democracy arrived “once and for all” in Greece, especially Periclean Athens. The phrasing presents the single-origin account as a quasi-miraculous descent rather than a demonstrated history.
- **SC:** Since the eighteenth century, European accounts of later democratic beginnings have repeatedly defined themselves through a dialogue with Athens.
- **EX:** Italian national memory foregrounds the communal movements of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries.
- **EX:** English national memory foregrounds the House of Commons and the regicidal rupture of beheading a king.
- **EX:** French national memory foregrounds the radical break of 1789.
- **SC:** These national traditions are historically respectable, but their historians defend them as nationally bounded inheritances.
- **SC:** Historians commonly treat chronological difference as a sufficient reason to avoid comparison.
- **SC:** Detienne suggests that the deeper reason for avoiding comparison is custodianship of national memory: writers of History act as guards over inheritances that comparison might disturb.
- **AI:** The object under critique is not national history as such. It is the conversion of national chronology and professional guardianship into a boundary on what may be compared.
- **PI:** Polymyth comparison should treat a tradition’s claim to unique or inaugural status as a claim to test, while preserving the tradition’s local chronology and self-description.

### Printed p. 80 / PDF p. 95 — “Multiple beginnings”

- **SC:** Historians of politics and political theorists usually reduce ancient-modern comparison to value judgment, typified by asking whether Athens was “really” democratic.
- **SC:** Over two centuries, Athens, often filtered through Thucydides, became the sole ancient interlocutor deemed worthy of comparison with modern Atlantic democracies.
- **AI:** A binary authenticity test — “was it really democracy?” — suppresses the plurality of practices that could be compared before cases receive or lose the label.
- **EX:** The Italian communal movement involved dozens of towns over two centuries, including small communes in Tuscany and Venetia that chose distinct courses of collective life.
- **EX:** Ancient Greece contained several hundred small communities experimenting for more than three centuries with would-be egalitarian political forms.
- **EX:** From the eighth century BCE, small cities in Sicily and on the Black Sea devised their own procedures for deliberating and deciding common affairs.
- **EX:** The city associated with Draco and Solon was only one type among dozens of Greek cities possessing room to invent new communal practices.
- **SC:** The field of comparison had expanded during the preceding thirty years through historical and anthropological work on other societies and continents.
- **EX:** Historians of Ukraine and the Russian world recovered democratic practices among Cossacks from the fifteenth through seventeenth centuries.
- **EX:** Anthropologists studying kinship in southern Ethiopia encountered indigenous assembly practices that marked out “places for politics.”
- **EX:** Detienne situates the Ochollo in southern Ethiopia from at least the nineteenth century, while treating their assembly form as independent of the European and Greek cases.
- **SC:** Political evidence can emerge incidentally from research organized under another disciplinary category, here kinship.
- **AI:** The chapter undermines a canon in which one exemplary city represents democracy while hundreds of variant experiments are relegated to local history, ethnography, or prepolitical custom.
- **PI:** A Polymyth corpus should preserve multiple instances and variations instead of allowing the most canonized narrative to stand in for an entire form.

### Printed p. 81 / PDF p. 96 — independent invention and “Concrete practices”

- **SC:** “Places for politics” have been invented repeatedly by societies widely separated in time and space.
- **EX:** The Ochollo did not consult the communal archives of Siena or Arezzo.
- **EX:** Fifteenth-century Cossacks did not need the *Iliad* or the archaeological plan of Megara Hyblaea to discover the agora or circular assembly.
- **EX:** Although the French Constituent Assembly knew something of the English parliamentary system, its members wished and needed to invent their new procedures in the practical vacuum left by the Ancien Régime.
- **SC:** The recurrence of analogous practices should not be explained through “miracles,” whether Greek, Ochollo, or Cossack.
- **SC:** Detienne calls for small, collaborative groups of historian-anthropologists and anthropologist-historians to compare political inventions as human possibilities rather than possessions of a central institution of scholarly expertise.
- **SC:** He associates methodological freedom with historians willing to work beyond the professional or disciplinary “order” governing them.
- **AI:** Detienne’s anti-diffusion examples are rhetorically strong but do not by themselves prove the absence of every indirect transmission. Their secure point is that diffusion must not be presumed merely because forms resemble one another.
- **SC:** The comparative exercise has two purposes: analyze experiences of beginning and study concrete assembly practices.
- **SC:** A beginning must not be confused with an origin. Beginnings are analytically useful because they reveal less consolidated, more open configurations than mature institutions.
- **SC:** Beginnings let observers trace empirical processes: acquisition of public speech rights, ceremonial positioning, selection of gathering place, and elementary rules through which a community represents itself.
- **SC:** These elementary practices sketch the developing sovereignty of the group.
- **PI:** The distinction between beginning and origin supports comparison without requiring a single genealogy, primordial archetype, or diffusion chain.
- **PI:** The minimal comparative unit should be a practice or relation — entry, speech, turn, vote, publicity, allocation, enforcement — before the analyst compares total institutions or inherited labels.

### Printed p. 82 / PDF p. 97 — practice before authority; “Communal affairs”

- **SC:** Detienne deliberately distances the project from typologies of “authority” or “legitimate power” derived from mature institutions.
- **SC:** His starting point is the practice of assembling in societies unconnected to one another.
- **EX:** The comparison includes Homeric warriors deliberating at the threshold of the earliest Greek cities, the Ochollo’s near-daily forum meetings, and the French Constituent Assembly’s incessant work on parliamentary autonomy and a new political form.
- **SC:** Deliberation about communal affairs differs categorically from conversation among people whose places in a social hierarchy remain fixed and unquestioned.
- **SC:** In societies without a legal State or the nineteenth-century Western Nation, “the common” may initially become apprehensible through shared war booty or collective landownership.
- **SC:** The right to speak in a deliberative assembly may be conceived in relation to the right to fight in war.
- **SC:** Some warrior societies appear to have discovered excellence in combat and excellence in council together.
- **SC:** In hierarchical urban societies, a *communitas* could emerge gradually around the practical management of a town or defense of a territory.
- **EX:** Medieval Italian urban and rural communes exemplify the progressive construction of such a *communitas*.
- **SC:** Recurrent assembly does not immediately produce a general will, popular sovereignty, or even a conscious explanation of group solidarity.
- **AI:** By bracketing institutional authority, Detienne makes emergent practice visible, but he also risks underdescribing coercion, inherited status, and exclusion already operating inside an ostensibly new assembly.
- **PI:** Any Polymyth account of “the common” should track the material event or practice that produces it instead of assuming that communal identity or shared purpose pre-exists the story’s action.

### Printed p. 83 / PDF p. 98 — Cossack community and Greek settlement

- **SC:** Cossack society probably did not originate as an aristocratic formation.
- **SC:** The Cossack concept of community, *tovarystvo*, was intertwined with collective property, common food, equality of speech, and equal access to official responsibilities.
- **SC:** Detienne proposes that the warrior function produced an egalitarian society rapidly, while its conception of speech remained less developed than its conception of war.
- **SL:** The initial formation of the Cossack assembly circle remains unknown because the foundational actions of the community of “free men” are undocumented.
- **SC:** Greek evidence permits closer observation of the creation of cities with deliberative assemblies and early vocabularies of common affairs.
- **EX:** Around 750-730 BCE, mobile groups in Asia Minor, Sicily, and the Peloponnese became conscious of themselves as groups while establishing assembly procedures wherever they settled.
- **SC:** Detienne pairs two near-contemporary, mirror-like sources for politics in formation: an archaeological-monumental assembly space and an epic assembly made through words and gestures.
- **EX:** The settlers of Megara Hyblaea reserved a central area for an agora when laying out their future city, even though the agora was constructed three generations later.
- **EX:** They simultaneously reserved a sacred sector for temples that would also be built later.
- **EX:** They divided the territory into approximately regular and equal holdings, probably allocated by lot.
- **AI:** The sequence matters: the plan and reservation of common/sacred space can precede monumental construction by generations. Built form is evidence of a prior political projection, not necessarily the moment politics began.
- **AI:** The Cossack case exposes a persistent limit of beginnings research: the configuration is theoretically most open at its beginning precisely where documentation may be thinnest.
- **PI:** A narrative can encode political becoming through spatial reservation, allocation, and repeated procedure before it articulates an abstract theory of community.

### Printed p. 84 / PDF p. 99 — archaeology, epic, and the semantics of *agora*

- **EX:** Detienne compares Megara Hyblaea with the *Odyssey*’s compressed city-founding image of Nausithoos, who leaves the assembly-less Cyclopes and founds a Phaeacian polis with agora, walls, temples, and distributed plots.
- **SC:** The Phaeacian vignette associates civilized political form with the conjunction of assembly, defense, sacred institutions, and land distribution.
- **EX:** The archaeological agora is paired with the functioning agoras of the armed Achaeans before Troy and the people of Ithaca.
- **EX:** The Greeks at Troy marked an agora near the center of their military camp.
- **SC:** In epic vocabulary, *agora* names three inseparable things: the place of assembly, the assembled men, and the public discourse exchanged there.
- **SC:** The assembly place incorporates an altar that orients it.
- **SC:** The assembled political body is composed of armed men deliberating.
- **SC:** In singular or plural form, *agora* can mean clashes of discourse or debates occurring in assembly.
- **SC:** Epic and later usage does not use *agora* for chatter, informal speech, or private conversation.
- **SC:** From its appearance, the agora is directed toward debate over the assembled group’s common affairs.
- **SC:** Public speech requires a prescribed ritual: the assembly forms a circle or semicircle; a prospective speaker advances to the middle and grasps the *skeptron*, which authorizes his advice or opinion.
- **SC:** Authorized speech must concern public business, *demion ti*, affecting the collectivity as a whole.
- **SC:** In principle, each assembled participant possesses the same right to speak.
- **AI:** “In principle” matters because the relevant collectivity consists of armed men. Equality inside the circle coexists with a strong membership boundary outside it.
- **AI:** The epic-archaeological pairing is comparativist evidence, not proof that the literary scene directly records Megara Hyblaea or any one historical assembly.
- **PI:** Polymyth analysis can compare narrative scenes with material configurations while keeping evidence classes separate: archaeology can establish spatial acts, while epic can establish a culturally available representation of those acts.

### Printed p. 85 / PDF p. 100 — “Fashioning a place in which to speak”

- **SC:** Greek gestures, rituals, and spatial forms make public speech on common affairs possible and help create the new assembly space itself.
- **SC:** The agora’s architectural form appears to be shaped by speech focused on what is equally common to those present.
- **SC:** Detienne compares this Greek case with other would-be egalitarian and deliberative societies through equally elementary practices.
- **EX:** When the Third Estate gathered in 1789, the first problem was spatial.
- **EX:** The common hall for the three Orders was organized around a raised platform for the king’s throne and court, with secretaries of state at a large table below it.
- **EX:** Clergy and nobility had upholstered benches, while the Third Estate sat on bare wooden benches.
- **EX:** The hall had no tiered seating and was poorly suited for intelligible deliberation among roughly 1,200 deputies.
- **SC:** Its inherited architecture was designed for listening to and contemplating authority, not for reciprocal debate and intervention.
- **EX:** After the Third Estate constituted itself as the National Assembly, it remained in the hall but had to deliberate amid spatial confusion.
- **EX:** Patrick Brasart’s analysis shows that the French initially lacked experience of genuine parliamentary debate.
- **EX:** Deputies had to invent an agenda, a designated speaking position, committees and commissions, ordered speaker lists, and lists of matters for debate.
- **AI:** Institutional declaration alone did not make the Third Estate an effective deliberative body. Procedures and spatial affordances had to be made congruent with its claim to national representation.
- **PI:** A coherence test for any represented assembly is whether the scene’s material layout, speaking procedure, and decision process can actually sustain the sovereignty the narrative assigns it.

### Printed p. 86 / PDF p. 101 — revolutionary geometry and practice-led rediscovery

- **EX:** On October 11, 1791, Quatremère de Quincy successfully proposed that the hall be circular or elliptical so every member could see every other member.
- **SC:** This first explicit proposal for an egalitarian space was justified through mutual visibility and audibility.
- **SC:** A deputy connected poor acoustics to shouting, bodily strain, proneness to violence, and contagion of that disposition among listeners.
- **EX:** A committee chose a semielliptical hall with the president at the center and tiered semicircular seating around him.
- **SL:** Even after the general geometry was settled, the optimal relation among speaker’s rostrum, assembly, and presiding officer remained unresolved.
- **SC:** Speaking from the center produced a form of French revolutionary eloquence different from the English Commons.
- **EX:** In the Commons, each member had spoken from his own place since the seventeenth century and addressed the Speaker.
- **SC:** The Constituent Assembly shows assembly practices producing a space fitted to deliberation among national representatives with equal rights to pronounce on the Good of the People.
- **SC:** In this configuration, “the People” is on the way to becoming the sovereign power through representational and procedural practice.
- **SL:** Detienne explicitly says this was neither democracy’s first experiment nor its last.
- **SC:** Over two years, the French reinvented the circular and theatrical deliberative space associated with egalitarian aspiration.
- **SC:** Cultural memory of Plutarch, antiquity, theatres, and ancient assembly places did not automatically furnish the needed form.
- **SC:** The form had to be rediscovered along a path generated by current practices rather than retrieved ready-made from memory.
- **AI:** Circle and ellipse are treated as enabling equality, not as sufficient guarantees of equality. Office placement, speaking order, acoustics, membership, and decision rules still structure the distribution of power.
- **PI:** Shared narrative memory does not itself reproduce a social form. A form becomes operative through reenacted procedures adapted to a present configuration.

### Printed p. 87 / PDF p. 102 — “A democracy invented in Africa”

- **SC:** Daily assembly practice makes southern Ethiopian cases unexpected within inherited accounts of African politics.
- **SL:** Detienne allows that comparable African experiments may have existed without attracting travelers’ or observers’ attention.
- **EX:** Marc Abélès went to the Gamo Mountains to map kinship and encountered organized assembly spaces at the subquarter, quarter, and plenary levels.
- **SC:** The Ochollo deliberate on common affairs among “citizens.”
- **EX:** The earlier citizen category included men and boys after puberty.
- **EX:** Plenary assemblies are prepared and convened by ad hoc figures and held within a circle of standing stones shaped as seats.
- **EX:** A person seeking to speak obtains permission from assembly presidents, steps into the circle, and faces the assembly.
- **SC:** The Ochollo case is presented as an autonomous invention rather than an imitation of the Ithacan agora.
- **SC:** In a society without autocratic chiefdom or royal power, the assembly appears to be the sole political place and is described as open to all.
- **EX:** Women had formerly spoken only from the edge of the male circle; they later became full citizens and exercised equal speech rights, aided by the socialist advance of the Addis Abeba regime.
- **EX:** Formal opening and closing rituals include dignitaries scattering fresh grass, blessing the assembly, and praying for productivity.
- **SC:** Plenary deliberation is limited to matters that concern all Ochollo.
- **SL:** Detienne leaves the historical mechanism of Ochollo egalitarianism unresolved and asks whether their former warrior organization resembled the Cossack or Homeric cases.
- **AI:** The claim that the assembly is “open to all” must be read diachronically. The same paragraph documents earlier gendered peripheralization and an age threshold, followed by a politically contingent expansion.
- **AI:** The external socialist regime complicates a simple indigenous/autochthonous story: later membership rules changed through an encounter between local practice and wider political transformation.
- **PI:** Boundaries must be treated as historical variables. A circle can preserve a visible center/periphery distinction while membership and speech rights migrate across it.

### Printed p. 88 / PDF p. 103 — “Assembling and giving a form to the *res publica*”

- **SC:** Detienne rejects the once-common equation “all societies are political” plus “politics is power.”
- **EX:** He frames the change polemically through the annual incorporation of May 1968 into French heritage, after which the formerly commonplace equation appears forgotten.
- **SC:** Anthropology and history need not organize political thought around the binary between stateless societies and societies oppressed by the State.
- **SC:** Concentrating on assembly practices reveals how different experiments generate representations of common affairs.
- **SC:** Phenomenological philosophies of communication and public space are legitimate comparison material for an experimental comparativist.
- **SC:** A spatial dimension of human being appears self-evident to a contemporary European formed by modern communication.
- **SC:** Heideggerian *Dasein* implies coexistence and a shared world but treats absorption in anonymous “being with” as a danger to authentic existence.
- **EX:** Detienne notes Heidegger’s critique of radio-era communication, anonymity, leveling, and degraded publicity, then wryly observes that the philosopher could not foresee the Internet.
- **SC:** On that phenomenological account, publicity threatens authenticity, creating a bleak foundation for politics because politics depends on the public domain.
- **AI:** The philosophical detour is not offered as a transhistorical truth. Detienne treats it as one culturally situated “indigenous theory” to place beside others.
- **PI:** Polymyth comparison can include formal theory as one culture’s self-interpretation without granting it authority over practices observed elsewhere.

### Printed p. 89 / PDF p. 104 — open public space, philosophical Greeks, and the nonuniversality of assembly

- **SC:** More optimistic phenomenologists derive communality from the original relation between “I” and “you.”
- **SC:** On this account, a possible politics must prevent the resulting “us” from enclosing itself within a closed communal household.
- **SC:** Detienne infers, with reference to Aboriginal discourse, that a public political space may require avoiding communities closed in on themselves.
- **SC:** Philosophical theories claiming radicality repeatedly return to selectively chosen Greeks: Heraclitus, Aristotle, or an abstract/personal city.
- **SC:** Philosophers render these Greeks untouchable by anchoring them in primordial archaicity beyond historical interpretation or verification.
- **AI:** The critique is epistemic as well as historical: the primordial Greek becomes an authority precisely by being insulated from the evidentiary tests applied to ordinary comparative cases.
- **SC:** Phenomenological models should be compared as seriously as indigenous theories from distant or past societies, but neither should replace analysis of actual practices.
- **SC:** The operative question is situated: what does assembling mean for each observable society?
- **SC:** Detienne refuses to ground the comparison in a general theory of innate human sociability.
- **SC:** Human gathering was not evidently a singular evolutionary turning point comparable to upright posture.
- **SC:** Not every group or community immediately adopts assembly.
- **AI:** Similarity of human sociality cannot explain the specific emergence of deliberative assembly; an account needs the intervening decisions, procedures, and material arrangements.
- **PI:** A Polymyth framework should resist allowing an abstract philosophical model to certify the meaning of all stories. The model itself belongs in the comparison as a situated artifact.

### Printed p. 90 / PDF p. 105 — will, assembly, common interest, and collective form

- **SC:** Assembly entails a prior decision. If it is not commanded by sovereign authority, it arises from the wills of group members.
- **EX:** People may assemble for war, hunting, festival, or ritual.
- **SC:** Detienne isolates the deliberately willed practice of assembling in a predetermined place to speak together.
- **SC:** Such practices are usually initiated by a few participants more motivated than the remainder.
- **EX:** The Constituent Assembly’s “general will” was a philosophical representation constructed and accepted in the eighteenth century.
- **EX:** The king originally ordered the nobility, clergy, and Third Estate to assemble as three Orders.
- **EX:** When the Third Estate declared itself the National Assembly, it claimed to cease being a mere collection of individuals and to embody the will of the Nation.
- **SC:** Political assembly exceeds generic coexistence, shared dwelling, or feasting because it gives a form to speech directed toward the group’s common interests.
- **SL:** A small active minority may assume responsibility for identifying those supposedly common interests.
- **SC:** Repeated assembly practices give form to a representation of the collectivity.
- **EX:** The resulting self-representations include Cossack “community,” Italian *universitas*, and the Greek “common hearth” and “common affairs.”
- **SC:** There is more than one way to establish a public thing, the Roman *res publica*.
- **SC:** Detienne contrasts these collective projects with the modern quest for individual authenticity, which he satirically calls a luxury of industrial and credit-card society.
- **AI:** The chapter’s sarcasm about commercialized authenticity is suggestive but is not supported here by a developed historical or sociological argument.
- **AI:** Voluntary assembly at the level of the whole group can coexist with agenda formation by an energetic minority. “Willed” therefore needs to be disaggregated into convening, attendance, proposal, deliberation, and decision.
- **PI:** The difference between a crowd, a commanded gathering, a ritual congregation, and a political assembly is narratively legible through purpose, voluntariness, speech form, and authority over the result.

### Printed p. 91 / PDF p. 106 — materializing the public thing

- **SC:** Italian citizens worked toward *communitas* through multiple practical experiments rather than a single founding declaration.
- **EX:** Militias guarded gates, ramparts, and other vital points.
- **EX:** Communes regulated routes of access to connect public spaces with communication networks.
- **EX:** Some communes eventually built a communal palace overlooking a space where they could stage and celebrate communal success and Good Government.
- **SC:** Cossack egalitarian assembly was reinforced by drawing lots for booty and parcels of land assigned to free men.
- **EX:** The Cossack circle displayed a banner and a long pole topped by a copper sphere and horse tails at its center.
- **EX:** Elected executives carried differentiated emblems: the war leader’s mace, judges’ seal, and secretary’s silver inkwell.
- **SC:** Egalitarian membership did not erase offices, differentiated functions, executive authority, or symbolic hierarchy; these were displayed inside the common circle.
- **SC:** In archaic Greece, assembly and booty-distribution practices jointly generated a representation of common business.
- **EX:** The scepter-bearing orator and victorious warriors depositing booty both moved to the assembly’s central point.
- **SC:** The booty deposited there became a common fund, linguistically connected to a term that would acquire the political sense of city or place of politics.
- **SC:** Warfare and debate shared an egalitarian spatial model centered on a group becoming conscious of itself and making sovereign decisions about its own affairs.
- **AI:** Several egalitarian configurations in the chapter are materially enabled by war, conquest, booty, territorial defense, or confiscation. Internal equality may therefore rest on external violence or exclusion.
- **PI:** Symbols and offices should be read relationally: an emblem’s placement, bearer, and movement through common space may reveal how a narrative reconciles equality with specialized authority.

### Printed p. 92 / PDF p. 107 — “Notaries, scribes, and messengers: Modes of publicity”

- **SC:** A political space requires publicity as well as a public place.
- **SC:** Kant’s now-obsolete sense of publicity meant making something public through material printing so it could be freely discussed.
- **SC:** Eighteenth-century print enabled diffusion of opinions and debates; without it, public opinion and the general will would remain rumor and politically ineffective.
- **SC:** Print cannot serve as the universal model of publicity because it is historically incongruous for Italian communes, Cossacks, and Ochollo.
- **SC:** Each of those societies nevertheless treats rendering public as essential to assembly.
- **EX:** Cossack documents and decisions were read aloud at the assembly place, strengthening the public character of the place itself.
- **EX:** Cossack writing appears symbolically in the great silver inkwell.
- **EX:** In Italian communes, notaries working with consuls introduced Roman law and made minutes of early assemblies.
- **EX:** Notarial writing formalized laypeople’s rights and authorized delegation, thereby publicizing powers claimed by the commune.
- **SC:** Civic squares and palaces later regarded as the natural stage of communal power often appeared only gradually, sometimes two centuries after assembly and council practices began.
- **EX:** Earlier Italian assemblies used provisional spaces associated with cathedrals or cemeteries.
- **SC:** Detienne judges this discontinuous Italian publicity less effective than the recurrent Ochollo assemblies.
- **EX:** Italian communal squares and palaces were erected mainly in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, often on property confiscated from defeated families.
- **AI:** Publicity is medium-specific: voice, messenger, minutes, inscription, print, recurring presence, and monumental space create different reach, persistence, accessibility, and authority.
- **AI:** The notary and secretary are boundary figures. They make collective decisions durable and delegable, while gaining control over the official form in which the collective can know its own acts.
- **PI:** Polymyth analysis should ask how common knowledge is produced inside the story-world. A public decision that lacks a credible channel of publicity cannot coherently bind the represented collectivity.

### Printed p. 93 / PDF p. 108 — Ochollo publicity and sovereign closure

- **EX:** Ochollo public places develop across quarters and subquarters as regular components of inhabited space and prepare matters for plenary assembly.
- **EX:** The plenary meets at Bekero, the oldest public place, located atop the crag overlooking Ochollo territory.
- **SC:** Bekero is the principal site of political speech; consensus decisions there implicate the Ochollo as a whole.
- **EX:** Messengers reinforce plenary publicity by implementing common decisions that possess the force of law.
- **SC:** A decision becomes fully public through the relation among open place, plenary attendance, consensus, and messenger enforcement.
- **SL:** Detienne says available information about West Africa is patchy and uncertain.
- **SC:** With that caveat, he contrasts Ochollo public space with West African configurations in which no independent space appears between king/chieftain and clan-lineage society.
- **SC:** A king concentrates powers otherwise distributed among clans and lineages.
- **SC:** Sacred kingship closes the gap between the sovereign’s prohibited person and a society that authorizes him to unite living beings with the visible and invisible forces of nature.
- **SC:** Royal authority is inseparable from elaborate power rituals that require far more force and energy than participation in Ochollo assemblies.
- **AI:** This is the chapter’s strongest negative structural comparison: assembly politics requires an institutionally distinct place where society can appear to itself; personalized sovereignty absorbs that space into the ruler.
- **AI:** “Spontaneous” or seemingly natural public places should not be read as literally unmade. The chapter elsewhere shows that recurrent use, ritual, hierarchy of places, and messengers actively reproduce them.
- **PI:** A spatial boundary between ruler, lineage, and public is politically consequential. Narrative settings can show whether “the people” possess an independent site of appearance or exist only through a sovereign person.

### Printed p. 94 / PDF p. 109 — “Writing down the law and making it speak”

- **EX:** Detienne characterizes the earliest Greek civic bodies as very small, commonly no more than roughly 200-500 citizens gathered in assembly.
- **SC:** Early Greek assembly practices benefited from the absence of sovereign palace figures like those of Mycenae and the Near East.
- **SC:** Greek mythology inherited sovereign figures through Near Eastern traditions, but early civic assemblies did not have to depose an equivalent living king.
- **EX:** The Homeric warriors had chosen Agamemnon as leader; they could form an agora and recognize reciprocal speech rights without regicide.
- **SC:** In Greece, publicity appears from the start as inseparable from the assembly space in which “the public” itself is defined.
- **SC:** Agora plus will to assemble means that full citizens directly participate in political matters.
- **EX:** Early documents describe citizens deliberating, deciding, and counting votes in the agora.
- **EX:** In Crete, adoption and acceptance of goods in settlement of disputed inheritance were publicly announced before assembled citizens.
- **EX:** Under Draco’s rule around 620 BCE, homicide charges were made in the agora.
- **SC:** Public homicide law and courts enlarged public space and strengthened politics.
- **SC:** Cities legislated in the corporate voice of their citizenry when a community member was killed.
- **EX:** Between about 620 and 530 BCE, homicide laws were displayed in large colored letters on stelae in Argos, Athens, Sicily, and Magna Graecia.
- **SC:** Murder affected the whole *polis*, or common thing, which determined reparation owed to family and community.
- **SC:** Civic solidarity came to operate beyond family discipline and a patron’s protection.
- **SC:** Law recast the killer as an accountable agent and differentiated voluntary, involuntary, premeditated, and legitimate killing under schemes associated with Draco and Zaleucus.
- **SC:** The city created a public judgment space in which citizens served as jurors and accuser and accused confronted one another politically.
- **AI:** The jurisdictional movement is from private retaliation or patronal protection toward a collectivity claiming authority to categorize violence and judge it publicly.
- **PI:** A narrative’s law becomes political when injury to one person is represented as affecting the common order and activating a public procedure beyond household or patron.

### Printed p. 95 / PDF p. 110 — inscription and vocalization

- **SC:** Greeks could have preserved an oral assembly culture like the Ochollo but instead adopted alphabetic writing as an instrument of publicity.
- **SC:** In the civic sequence Detienne describes, citizens speak one after another about the city’s affairs before decisions are fixed and publicized.
- **SC:** Writing made assembly decisions and shared rules visible and durable for citizens who learned a relatively small alphabet.
- **SC:** The relevant written words were sanctioned by the assembly majority.
- **EX:** In Rome, proclamations read aloud made law effective.
- **EX:** In 1791-1792, Marseilles Jacobins tried to preserve the citizen’s original right to declare law by appointing “the best among them” to make law speak at patriotic gatherings.
- **EX:** Around 650 BCE, Dreros inscribed a ten-year prohibition on someone again serving as *cosmos*, its highest magistrate.
- **EX:** Chios inscribed a rule fixing the assembly two days after Apollo’s festival.
- **SC:** These inscriptions were political because they materially publicized concrete meeting rules and constraints on office.
- **EX:** Another formula assigned proposal to the council and final decision to the assembly majority.
- **SC:** By at least the seventh century BCE, inscription on varied materials organized public space and helped shape policies produced through assembly.
- **AI:** Public writing does not eliminate mediation. Literacy, control of inscription, selection of “the best” declarers, and the council’s proposal power create new offices and thresholds inside publicity.
- **AI:** Making law visible and making law speak are complementary operations: durability does not guarantee accessibility, and oral proclamation does not guarantee persistence.
- **PI:** Media should be treated as constitutive elements of a political or narrative form, not as neutral containers carrying an unchanged collective decision.

### Printed p. 96 / PDF p. 111 — voluntarist sovereignty and rare clearings of equality

- **SC:** Early Greek regimes followed a voluntarist model: rules from routine to ambitious were explicitly sanctioned as the conscious will of the civic group.
- **EX:** Civic formulas such as “we, the city” and “it pleased the council and assembly” made collective authorization explicit.
- **SC:** Publicized decisions displayed in highly visible and symbolically charged sites cultivated the group’s representation of sovereignty over itself.
- **EX:** Relevant display sites included agora, acropolis, and major sanctuary.
- **SC:** Historically attested places of equality are rare, described as clearings in a forest of severe inequalities and naturalized hierarchies.
- **SL:** Detienne says knowledge of how divine kingship and other sovereignties were invented remains limited.
- **SC:** Assertions that equal rights are established by nature are historically uncommon and strongly tied to particular cultures.
- **SC:** Assembly procedures reveal gradual transformations in the notion of the individual.
- **EX:** Cossack and Ochollo assemblies contrast with many societies across China, India, and Africa in which gatherings cannot disregard kinship, lineage, or caste hierarchy.
- **EX:** Any Cossack could request an assembly, and any Ochollo could speak within his circle.
- **SC:** Assembly speech provides a path by which an orator may become a respected counselor, war leader, or community dignitary.
- **AI:** Detienne’s sweeping China-India-Africa contrast is a high-level generalization unsupported by claim-level citation in this chapter and should not be imported as a verified universal.
- **AI:** Equality of access can generate distinction through performance: the common right to speak may produce recognized leaders rather than abolish hierarchy.
- **PI:** A Polymyth role can be both structurally open and narratively selective: anyone may occupy it in principle, while the story elevates particular figures through demonstrated speech or action.

### Printed p. 97 / PDF p. 112 — non-equivalent equalities, decision rules, and minority protection

- **SL:** A will to deliberate on common affairs does not inevitably produce individual consent, rights, or duties.
- **SC:** Voluntary assembly nevertheless tends to favor people represented as free and egalitarian.
- **SC:** Drawing lots in warrior circles may foster self-representation as equivalent persons because participants receive approximately equal shares.
- **SC:** In the eighteenth century, election displaced lot because representation and government required legitimation by consenting individuals.
- **SC:** Lot does not express individual will, but resemblance and similarity still contribute to models of equality.
- **SC:** Spartan recognition of one another as “the same,” probably formed through warrior training, differs from legal equality or an arithmetically equal distribution of rights.
- **SC:** Warfare and council excellence changed as representations of common affairs and the Common Good changed.
- **SC:** Majority voting encouraged the representation of an abstract individual that supported equality before law.
- **EX:** Sparta’s assembly of “the same” operated by consensus, as do the Ochollo and as did the Cossack circle.
- **SC:** Individual ballots, secrecy or openness of voting, majority definition, and quorum are decisive design elements once an autonomous political space and its jurisdiction exist.
- **SC:** A later reaction against majority violence could assign the minority responsibility for protecting what Pierre Bayle called the universal rights of private conscience.
- **SC:** Assembly procedures urgently raise the relation between models of citizenship, recognized individual qualities, and qualities the community attributes to its political place.
- **AI:** The chapter supplies no linear ladder from lot to vote to rights. Each procedure constructs a different political person and can secure one equality while violating another.
- **AI:** Consensus can conceal pressure, and majority voting can enact majoritarian violence; Detienne notes the second danger but does not test either procedure in detail here.
- **PI:** “Equality” should be decomposed into membership, speech, share, vote, legal status, eligibility, consent, visibility, and protection from majority power before comparing stories or institutions.

### Printed p. 98 / PDF p. 113 — civic personhood, virtue, contingency, and disciplinary critique

- **SC:** Political places bear many names — *universitas*, public hearth, sovereign people, common affairs, human affairs — and each name relates a community’s ideal person to its collective form.
- **SC:** Early Greek cities quickly defined qualities expected of speakers and citizens committed to living well together.
- **SC:** Equality and interchangeability were deemed insufficient; citizens also needed uprightness, virtue, and a civic nobility, captured by Simonides’ “foursquare” person.
- **SC:** The formula “the city makes the man” expressed the city’s formative and educational role.
- **SL:** Greek politics never fully took education over as a public function.
- **SC:** Good Government was imagined to require particular personal qualities.
- **EX:** Jacobins likewise sought a virtuous citizen able both to represent and recognize popular sovereignty.
- **SC:** Private interest and self-indulgent individualism appeared as threats to democratic judgment and competence.
- **SC:** Jacobin egalitarian politics called individuals to oppose the privileges and hierarchies of the Ancien Régime.
- **SC:** Every egalitarian political system confronts the problem of the “remarkable citizen” who distinguishes himself through assembly practices and is expected to possess special qualities.
- **SC:** Comparative observation reveals practices that might never have been adopted, might have produced different equalities, or might disappear.
- **SC:** Some long-lived achievements may have become possible through the disappearance of alternative experiments now forgotten.
- **SC:** School and university history teaches “our” Greeks and largely excludes more modern peoples and their practices from the same comparative field.
- **SC:** Anthropology retains a habitual freedom to cross cultures and look for the will to assemble.
- **AI:** Civic virtue criteria can answer the real problem of office and judgment while also becoming a new boundary policed by those authorized to define virtue, competence, or nobility.
- **AI:** The chapter explicitly critiques curricular canon and disciplinary selection; it does not directly analyze diplomas, professional licensure, degree inflation, or credential screening. Any application to credentialism must therefore remain a second-order inference about authority over valid knowledge and qualified participation.
- **PI:** Stories of egalitarian orders should be checked for the return of exceptional-person criteria: who defines the worthy speaker or citizen, and does that standard enrich common judgment or rebuild hereditary/credential hierarchy under another name?

### Printed p. 99 / PDF p. 114 — why compare?

- **SC:** Anthropology’s taste for dissonance directs attention toward contrasts that appear excessive or mysterious across barriers of time and space.
- **SC:** The discipline’s recurring anxiety about its own identity and future repeatedly provokes the question of why comparison is justified.
- **SC:** First answer: placing several experiments in perspective reveals zones of intelligibility that historians of politics and philosophers can recognize within their own fields.
- **SC:** Second answer: a collection of beginnings observed in concrete development permits microscopic analysis of neighboring configurations.
- **SC:** Each configuration’s differential features help identify the deviation that distinguishes one political microconfiguration among a larger series of possibilities.
- **AI:** “Deviation from the norm” is methodologically ambiguous unless the norm is constructed from the comparative field rather than silently supplied by Athens, Europe, the State, or democracy as a finished ideal.
- **PI:** A rigorous Polymyth comparison should generate its axes from recurring relations across cases, retain differential features, and show where each configuration diverges. It should not erase dissonance to make cases instances of a predetermined archetype.

## Mechanisms Detienne isolates

1. **Plural beginning without miracle:** recurrent forms become comparable human inventions without being reduced to a unique Greek source or unexplained civilizational exception. (pp. 79-81)
2. **Practice-to-community formation:** repeated assembly procedures generate a representation of the collectivity instead of merely expressing an already finished people. (pp. 81-82, 88-91)
3. **Common object formation:** booty, land, defense, urban access, murder, law, or public welfare gives participants something they can recognize as affecting the whole. (pp. 82-83, 90-95)
4. **Spatial affordance:** circle, center, tiering, visibility, hearing, altar, rostrum, and relation to the presiding office distribute participation and authority. (pp. 83-87)
5. **Ritual authorization:** scepter, permission from presidents, formal blessings, insignia, and procedural opening distinguish consequential public speech from private talk. (pp. 84, 87, 91)
6. **Material equality:** lot allocation and equal shares create representations of similarity that can precede or differ from rights-based equality. (pp. 83, 91, 97)
7. **War-council coupling:** capacity to fight and capacity to advise may emerge as linked claims to membership and excellence. (pp. 82-83, 91, 97)
8. **Publicity:** a political place requires a method for rendering its actions public through recurring presence, oral reading, messengers, minutes, inscription, proclamation, or print. (pp. 92-96)
9. **Persistence and abstraction through writing:** inscription detaches rules from an ephemeral meeting, materializes collective will, and supports limits on office, calendars, proposal rules, and majority authority. (pp. 94-96)
10. **Public jurisdiction:** treating homicide as injury to the common expands politics beyond household discipline and patronal protection and creates public categories, law, and judgment. (pp. 94-95)
11. **Procedure-to-person formation:** lot, vote, consent, consensus, public speech, and civic education generate different images of the individual and citizen. (pp. 96-98)
12. **Equal access producing distinction:** open speech permits some speakers to become counselors, leaders, or dignitaries; egalitarian procedure can generate earned asymmetry. (pp. 96-98)
13. **Practice before recollection:** the French did not retrieve assembly form directly from classical cultural memory; they rebuilt it through current procedural problems. (pp. 85-86)
14. **Infrastructure lag:** monumental public architecture can appear generations or centuries after the practices that it later seems naturally to embody. (pp. 83, 92)
15. **Sovereign closure:** where the king’s person absorbs social and cosmic unity, no autonomous gap exists in which the society may assemble and appear independently. (p. 93)
16. **Comparative differentiation:** neighboring cases expose component variables and show how a particular microconfiguration departs from other possible combinations. (pp. 98-99)

## Boundaries, authority, and institutional mediation

### Boundaries inside political configurations

- **Membership boundary:** armed man, free man, citizen, pubescent boy, woman, deputy, representative, lineage member, or caste position determines who can enter the political “all.” (pp. 84, 87, 90-91, 96-98)
- **Speech boundary:** the scepter, assembly presidents’ permission, rostrum position, agenda, speaker list, or selected law-declarer separates effective public speech from private talk or noise. (pp. 84-87, 95)
- **Spatial boundary:** center versus edge, circle versus royal platform, independent agora versus sovereign household, and public place versus cathedral/cemetery create different relations of presence. (pp. 83-87, 92-93)
- **Subject-matter boundary:** public speech must concern what affects the collectivity; not every conversation or dispute is automatically political. (pp. 82, 84, 87, 90)
- **Public/private boundary:** law and assembly move homicide, inheritance, adoption, opinion, and conscience into or against collective jurisdiction. (pp. 92, 94-95, 97)
- **Decision boundary:** council proposal, assembly majority, consensus, quorum, individual ballot, and minority protection define who can convert speech into binding action. (pp. 93, 95-97)
- **Temporal boundary:** office term limits, meeting calendars, ordered agenda, and durability of inscription make collective authority continuous across meetings. (pp. 85, 95)
- **Mediation boundary:** notary, consul, secretary, messenger, president, Speaker, judge, elected executive, and public orator connect collectivity to an authoritative record or act. (pp. 85-87, 91-95)
- **Community boundary:** Detienne distinguishes public political space from a communal “us” enclosed on itself and from mere coexistence or feasting. (pp. 89-90)
- **Jurisdictional boundary:** civic homicide law establishes claims beyond family discipline and patronal protection. (pp. 94-95)

### Academic and disciplinary authority

- **SC:** National historians guard memory and use chronology to block comparisons that might unsettle national ownership. (p. 79)
- **SC:** Political history and theory privilege Athens as the legitimate ancient comparator and reduce comparison to evaluative labeling. (p. 80)
- **SC:** Detienne calls for historian-anthropologist collaboration outside the monopoly of an “institutional centre of scholarly expertise.” (p. 81)
- **SC:** Research organized under the heading of kinship unexpectedly revealed a political assembly system, showing that disciplinary categories can hide relevant phenomena. (pp. 80, 87)
- **SC:** Philosophy repeatedly selects its own primordial Greeks and places them beyond historical verification. (p. 89)
- **SC:** School and university history reproduces possessive familiarity with “our Greeks” while omitting modern and noncanonical political experiments. (p. 98)
- **SC:** Anthropology’s comparative reach is productive, yet professional concern about the discipline and its future repeatedly makes comparison defend itself. (pp. 98-99)
- **AI:** These claims support a critique of epistemic credentialism in the limited sense that established offices, disciplines, and canons police which cases count as political knowledge and who may compare them.
- **AI:** They do not amount to a direct critique of degrees, academic hiring, diploma mills, peer review, licensure, or credential inflation. Those stronger credentialism claims require other evidence.
- **AI:** Detienne’s own chapter depends on specialized archaeological, philological, historical, and ethnographic expertise. His target is centralized or exclusionary authority over comparison, not evidence, trained inquiry, or expertise as such.

## Objections, tensions, and limits

### Explicitly acknowledged by Detienne

1. **Cossack founding evidence is absent:** the formation of the first assembly circle remains a mystery. (p. 83)
2. **Ochollo causal origin is unresolved:** Detienne asks whether warrior organization explains the form but supplies no conclusion. (pp. 87-88)
3. **Other African assemblies may have escaped observation:** absence from travel and ethnographic records is not evidence of absence. (p. 87)
4. **West African information is patchy and uncertain:** the king/clan contrast is therefore provisional. (p. 93)
5. **Assembly does not guarantee consent, rights, or duties:** the causal relationship is favorable, not inevitable. (p. 97)
6. **Different equalities are non-equivalent:** sameness, equal shares, legal equality, and distributed rights must remain distinct. (p. 97)
7. **Majority decisions can be violent:** minority/private-conscience protection may be required. (p. 97)
8. **Active minorities may define common interests:** collective speech does not erase agenda power. (p. 90)
9. **Equal citizenship is insufficient by itself:** every egalitarian system faces the contested demand for competent or virtuous participants. (p. 98)
10. **Alternative experiments disappear:** the visible historical outcome may depend on forgotten paths, limiting retrospective reconstruction. (p. 98)
11. **Royal-sovereignty origins remain poorly understood:** the chapter compares equality more confidently than it reconstructs the configurations it contrasts with equality. (p. 96)
12. **Spatial design remains contested:** even after choosing a semiellipse, the French still had to resolve the speaker-president-assembly relation. (p. 86)
13. **The French case is one experiment among others:** it is explicitly denied first or final status. (p. 86)

### Analytical objections or cautions raised here

1. **Similarity does not establish independent invention:** the anti-miracle and anti-diffusion argument is persuasive as method, but direct and indirect transmission still require case-specific evidence.
2. **Beginnings are open but evidentially fragile:** the stage Detienne most wants to observe often leaves the fewest records.
3. **Circle does not equal equality:** circular form can improve mutual visibility while preserving presidents, rostra, gendered edges, citizenship exclusions, offices, and agenda control.
4. **Internal equality may depend on external domination:** war booty, territorial seizure, defense, and confiscated property materially support several “egalitarian” spaces.
5. **The politics/power separation can obscure coercion:** bracketing authority typologies reveals constituent practice but may leave domination inside and outside the assembly underexamined.
6. **“Open to all” requires a defined all:** the Ochollo account itself shows age and historical gender limits, while Greek and Cossack cases center armed or free men.
7. **Publicity can generate gatekeepers:** literacy, notarial record, inscription, message transmission, and selected proclamation create asymmetries in access to the public act.
8. **Consensus and majority have different pathologies:** majoritarian violence is named, while consensus pressure, silence, and informal veto are not examined.
9. **Virtue can rebuild hierarchy:** a need for competence can become an exclusionary standard controlled by officeholders or credentialed interpreters.
10. **Regional generalizations outrun the chapter’s evidence:** especially the contrast spanning China, India, Africa, and West African kingship.
11. **The “common” may be authored by a minority:** Detienne admits this but does not give a procedure for distinguishing legitimate agenda leadership from capture.
12. **Media access is uneven:** visible inscription and print are political only for audiences able to encounter and interpret them; oral declaration solves a different access problem.
13. **Practice-led emergence can become teleological in retrospect:** phrases such as working “toward” *communitas* risk making later institutions appear as the implicit end of earlier experiments.
14. **Literary-material comparison needs evidence labels:** the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* reveal representations, while Megara Hyblaea reveals material planning; neither source can silently substitute for the other.
15. **The comparative norm is underspecified:** the concluding “deviation from the norm” could recreate the privileged center the chapter opposes unless the norm is explicitly constructed and revisable.
16. **Citation support is unusually thin:** one endnote names the core research lineage, but detailed claims across many cultures receive no claim-level documentation in this chapter.

## Provisional Polymyth relevance ledger

Every item below is an inference for consideration, not a canonical Polymyth rule or accepted project decision.

1. **Multiple beginnings over unique origin:** analogous narrative or institutional forms can recur without a single archetypal source. Comparison should test transmission, convergence, and independent invention rather than assume any one of them. (from pp. 79-81)
2. **Compare operations before total labels:** convening, entry, placement, speaking, contesting, deciding, publicizing, recording, enforcing, and remembering are more discriminating units than “democracy,” “community,” or “assembly.” (from pp. 81-82)
3. **Treat the common as produced:** stories may show a group becoming a group through shared danger, allocation, injury, ritual, or speech. The communal subject need not pre-exist the event. (from pp. 82-83, 90-91)
4. **Keep evidence classes explicit:** archaeological space, epic representation, legal inscription, ethnographic report, historical archive, and philosophical model support different kinds of claims. (from pp. 83-89, 94-96)
5. **Map geometry as meaning-bearing action:** center, edge, circle, platform, visibility, audibility, orientation, and movement can constitute political relations rather than decorate them. (from pp. 84-87)
6. **Audit the implied “all”:** whenever a narrative says the whole people, community, or assembly, identify exclusions by sex, age, status, arms, citizenship, caste, lineage, literacy, or office. (from pp. 84, 87, 96-98)
7. **Separate equalities:** equal presence, speech, share, vote, law, eligibility, consent, and protection are distinct variables and may conflict. (from pp. 96-97)
8. **Trace authorization objects and gestures:** scepter, insignia, rostrum, blessing, agenda, seal, inkwell, or inscription can reveal how speech becomes binding. (from pp. 84-87, 91-95)
9. **Require a publicity channel:** binding collective acts need an internally coherent route to common knowledge and implementation. (from pp. 92-96)
10. **Treat media changes as form changes:** voice, messenger, record, inscription, and print alter durability, reach, contestability, and who mediates the act. (from pp. 92-96)
11. **Test political claims against practical affordance:** a story’s declared equality or sovereignty may be incoherent if its space, acoustics, agenda, membership, or decision procedure makes collective deliberation impossible. (from pp. 85-86)
12. **Distinguish gathering types:** commanded assembly, feast, hunt, war band, ritual congregation, crowd, and deliberative public have different purposes and authority structures. (from pp. 89-90)
13. **Track the transition from household injury to common injury:** a narrative can mark political formation by relocating judgment beyond family, patron, or private retaliation. (from pp. 94-95)
14. **Analyze leadership generated by equality:** open participation can produce counselors and distinguished citizens without hereditary rank; the mechanism and its legitimacy still need scrutiny. (from pp. 96-98)
15. **Watch virtue become a boundary:** the “remarkable citizen” can solve a competence problem or reintroduce elite closure. A coherent account must show who defines and tests the quality. (from p. 98)
16. **Do not equate cultural memory with operative continuity:** remembered ancient forms may need to be invented again through present practice. (from pp. 85-86)
17. **Allow institutional layers to have different dates:** a practice, concept, monument, text, and later self-description may belong to different stages of the same configuration. (from pp. 83, 92)
18. **Preserve failed and vanished branches:** surviving institutions should not erase other experiments from the possibility space. (from p. 98)
19. **Compare configurations, not prestige:** the most canonized example should be one row in a differential field, not the source of every axis or the standard that decides authenticity. (from pp. 79-81, 98-99)
20. **Treat theory as a cultural object when appropriate:** Heideggerian publicness, Jacobin general will, and Greek civic formulas can be compared with indigenous theories without being installed as universal metalanguages. (from pp. 88-90)
21. **Preserve scalar differences:** a camp, mountain plenary, small polis, medieval commune, and 1,200-member national assembly can share operations while differing in scale, representation, frequency, and publicity. (whole chapter)
22. **Ask whether the public has an independent place:** where ruler, household, lineage, or closed community absorbs every collective relation, the story may lack a space in which a political body can appear as such. (from pp. 89, 93)
23. **Separate source thesis from project use:** Detienne supports comparative polygenesis and practice-centered analysis; he does not supply a completed Polymyth ontology, coherence standard, or credentialism theory. Those would require explicit project decisions and additional sources.
24. **Require downstream verification:** because the chapter’s sole endnote is a lineage note rather than claim-level apparatus, empirical details should be checked against Abélès, Brasart, Ruzé, archaeology, and relevant primary evidence before they are treated as verified Polymyth facts.
