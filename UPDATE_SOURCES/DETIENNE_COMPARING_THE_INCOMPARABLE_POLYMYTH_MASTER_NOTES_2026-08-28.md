## The book’s central method in compact form

1. **[D] Comparability is made through a question.** Objects do not arrive already sorted into comparable and incomparable classes. The analyst selects a relation, feature, gesture, object, practice, or problem and constructs a comparison around it. Ordinary declarations that only similar things may be compared hide an initial value judgment and protect familiar classifications. (pp. ix–xi, 22–23, 37–39)

2. **[D] Local reconstruction comes first.** Early anthropological comparison worked with sets of relations already detected within each culture. Detienne’s final Chapter 1 formulation asks the researcher to enter a cultural network deeply enough to think from within it while preserving enough distance to interpret it. (pp. x, 21)

3. **[D] Dissimilarity is an instrument.** Unrelated languages, distant territorial systems, variant historicities, and unconnected assembly practices can expose features that a specialist no longer sees in a familiar case. The gain comes through contrast and cognitive dissonance. (pp. 23–24)

4. **[D] The negative case is productive.** Japan and Vedic India resist the initial category of foundation. Their resistance disassembles the category into its concealed spatial, temporal, ritual, agentive, and territorial components. Incomparability becomes a diagnostic event. (pp. 25–28)

5. **[D] The unit of comparison is relational and small.** Detienne prefers microconfigurations, mechanisms, localized sequences, and plates of relative coherence. Themes and total civilizations are usually too coarse. A founder, god, boundary, past, or democracy gains meaning through the relations that organize it. (pp. 30–32, 59–71, 81–99)

6. **[D] Similarity can coexist with constitutive difference.** Several cases may share an orientation or operation while arranging their elements differently. The method asks how they are similar, where they diverge, and what the divergence reveals. It refuses to promote resemblance into identity. (pp. 30–32, 98–99)

7. **[D] Comparison is experimental in a qualified sense.** Analysts place a selected object, gesture, or situation into several configurations and observe how it reacts. Historical configurations cannot be repeated under controlled conditions, so Detienne ultimately prefers the language of manipulation and revisable testing. (pp. xiv–xv, 65–77)

8. **[D] Context constrains association.** A horse, threshold, foot, stone, road, altar, speaking object, or circle has indefinitely many possible meanings. Cultural knowledge of material, ritual, linguistic, ecological, technical, institutional, and historical relations constrains the comparison. (pp. 69–71)

9. **[D] Comparison requires collaboration.** The working unit is a small group of historian-anthropologists and anthropologist-historians who retain local competence, absorb one another’s questions, and meet regularly. Detienne treats sustained collective thought as more important than a large grant. (p. 24)

10. **[D] The purpose is interpretive gain and ethical displacement.** A successful comparison reveals an overlooked property, breaks a provincial common sense, pluralizes possible forms, and gives the analyst critical distance from inherited values and traditions. (pp. xiv–xv, 23, 38–39, 98–99)

## Current project/source relation after the ironman audit

### [U | PROJECT-CURRENT] Detienne is a point in favor of the always-already claim

The user's ruling is explicit: Detienne's method is a point in favor of the project's always-already claim. That project claim says a structural relation can operate before the project, analyst, or participants have named it. Recognition and comparison make the relation articulable; they do not have to create the relation's existence.

**[D | SOURCE-DIRECT]** Detienne argues that comparables are constructed through a declared question, bounded materials, selected relations, and experimental juxtaposition. He also shows that the resulting comparison can disclose an unnoticed property, hidden premise, or relation in the cases. (pp. ix–xv, 22–32, 59–78)

**[I | INFERENCE]** “Constructing a comparable” and “inventing the compared structure into existence” are different claims. Detienne's analyst constructs the comparative apparatus. The apparatus can reveal a relation that was already operating in each case. This is the precise route by which the book supports the user's always-already claim.

**[T | LIMIT]** Detienne does not use the project's term “always-already,” and the support does not make every proposed resemblance real. Each comparison still owes local reconstruction, a declared axis, load-bearing differences, resistant cases, and a null-result possibility.

### [U | PROJECT-CURRENT] Polycognate is a complex axiom that always survives

This is the user's current ruling and must remain exact. The earlier phrase “individual Polycognate claims” referred only to proposed applications such as whether **saudade** and **hiraeth** occupy the same structural position, or whether **Tatarigami** and the **Mind Flayer** belong in the same wounded-being chain. These examples explain the phrase. They create no new admission, defeat, or hierarchy rule. The project's existing admission, correction, and bidirectionality rules remain in force.

**[I | INFERENCE]** Detienne supplies stronger tests for particular pairings through relational reconstruction, concrete probes, counterconfigurations, and return to local specificity. These tests improve the use of the axiom. They do not create the project's categories, impose Detienne's vocabulary on them, or convert a rejected example into a defeat of the axiom.

### [U | PROJECT-CURRENT] The four-register update is already present

The current project compresses **gesture → ritual → idiom → narrative** and keeps **narrative → idiom → ritual → gesture** as a separate reverse activation. Detienne's relational and scale-sensitive comparison can support analysis within this already-current structure. This note neither replaces the four registers nor invents a new decision about reciprocity or hierarchy.

### Non-opposition-as-presented ruling on genealogy

**[U | PROJECT-CURRENT]** The fact that this book does not document a continuous genealogy from ancient boundary practices to contemporary therapeutic vocabulary does not oppose the user's claim as presented, because that continuous genealogy was not the claim being advanced in this exchange.

This is a narrow **non-opposition-as-presented** ruling. It is not compatibility, support, adoption, or proof. ## High-value findings for Polymyth

### Comparison and Mezo Logic

1. **[P] The proposed bridge needs a declared comparative question.** A pair of materials is never simply comparable. Record the exact relation under test: a practice, sequence, spatial operation, authority relation, figure-position, mode of action, temporal orientation, or other bounded mechanism.

2. **[P] Reconstruct each source system independently before cross-system work.** This strongly aligns with current Mezo Logic’s Stage 0–3 sequence: bound the corpus, reconstruct operations and limits, classify internal coherence, then test a bridge. Detienne supplies especially strong reasons for this order. (pp. x, 21, 24, 30–32)

3. **[P] Replace motif matching with configuration matching.** A shared founder, serpent, threshold, descent, council, or war god is only a candidate locator. The evidentiary unit is the relation among elements: who acts, upon what, through which gesture, under which constraint, with which result. (pp. 30–32, 60–71)

4. **[P] Add a category-strength test.** Before comparison, ask whether the initial category is too strong, too weak, or productive. A strong category classifies everything before discovery. A weak category groups cases without generating discriminating questions. A productive category produces connected questions and can be revised by resistant cases. (p. 25)

5. **[P] Add a negative-case protocol.** The first case that seems incomparable should receive focused analysis. Record which assumed component fails, which new variable appears, and whether the initial category should split, narrow, or disappear. (pp. 25–28)

6. **[P] Let the same case participate in multiple comparisons.** Japan, Vedic India, and South American forest societies enter different groupings when the axis changes from nonbeginning to treatment of remains or territorial centers. A source has no single permanent comparative identity. (pp. 30–31)

7. **[P] Keep observed grouping, reconstructed relation, and analyst-created juxtaposition distinct.** Shared altars and paired invocations are source-attested relations. A hypothesized divine function is reconstructed. A comparison across cultures is analyst-produced. Their evidence statuses should never merge. (pp. 60–71)

8. **[P] Give differences load-bearing status.** Two configurations can share an orientation while their internal interconnections differ. The difference is part of the finding. It prevents synthesis from collapsing into a generalized skeleton. (pp. 30–32)

9. **[P] A null comparison is a valid result.** An object or gesture may fail to generate an illuminating reaction. That outcome can show that the chosen variable is coarse, arbitrary, or source-contaminating. Detienne’s experimental framing implies this even though he gives no formal null-result protocol. (pp. xiv–xv, 69–77)

10. **[P] Rebuild demonstrates portability rather than truth.** A pattern that can be transferred into new narrative material has generative utility. Ontological reality, historical independence, genealogy, and causal force require separate evidence.

11. **[U] Current Polymyth Coherence already contains strong Detienne-compatible controls.** Version 6.10.0 bounds exact objects, keeps artifact substrate separate from interpretation, preserves branches, requires a post-reconstruction boundary challenge, differentiates relation types, requires negative controls for population claims, and treats surface resemblance as insufficient. Detienne reinforces these controls rather than replacing them.

12. **[T | RESOLVED-HISTORY] An older ML* sentence was materially looser than the current Coherence standard.** The older Comparative-archetypal formulation treated lack of coordination as proof of an archetypal pattern. The newest project versions have already corrected that shortcut through typed relations, transmission audit, source-contamination controls, and separate Mezo tests. Detienne supports the corrected narrower sequence: uncoordinated recurrence can support a scoped independent-convergence hypothesis only after the relevant audits and relational reconstruction.

13. **[P] Detienne strengthens the Strip operation.** Stripping should recover a relational microconfiguration rather than the lowest common denominator chosen from several examples. The retained structure needs constraints, internal orientation, and counterinstances. The discarded material needs a return pass so that local meaning re-enters the analysis.

14. **[P] Detienne strengthens the Notice operation.** Notice should begin with full bounded objects, explicit axes, and source-attested relations. Uncoordinated resemblance generates a candidate. It does not close the classification.

15. **[P] Detienne strengthens the Rebuild operation.** Rebuild should report what survived the substrate change, what broke, which source features were omitted, and whether the new case is correspondence, analogy, structural homology, adaptation, or a generative craft use.

### Academia, credentials, expertise, and institutional gatekeeping

1. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Academic credentials can become jurisdictional instruments.** Detienne describes historians as a credentialed corporation charged with national conscience, historical object definition, and the proper writing of France. (pp. 9–10, 17–18) **[I | INFERENCE]** In project terms, the mechanism is qualification expanding into authority over the field's permissible objects and questions.

2. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Credential control works through ordinary procedure.** Detienne describes research permits, peer judgment, chair appointments, examination systems, disciplinary titles, approved problem sets, and canonical curricula as mechanisms that can enforce enclosure while speaking in scientific or democratic language. (pp. 12–18) **[I | INFERENCE]** The book supports analysis of institutional procedure presenting itself as neutral. It does not establish a separate claim about meritocratic rhetoric on these pages.

3. **[D] Professional custody turns objects into property.** Greece, a field site, a historical period, a national past, and a corpus of canonical texts can become a preserve. Scholars then treat cross-field movement as trespass and position local expertise as exclusive ownership. (pp. x–xiii, 17–19, 36–38, 57–65)

4. **[D] Institutions reward closure.** Detienne links disciplinary partitions to chairs, honors, decorations, academies, career prospects, dissertation forms, and approval from academic superiors. Some motive claims are posed sarcastically or hypothetically and require separate biographical evidence. The structural incentive critique is explicit. (pp. xii–xiii, 64–65)

5. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Detienne connects the isolated boss-god, the dissertation monograph, and the academic boss in his polemic.** (pp. 62–65) **[I | INFERENCE]** The project can test whether a career-safe unit of analysis reproduces institutional hierarchy and makes relational or lateral powers secondary. The parallel is strongly implied, while its general institutional operation needs evidence beyond the analogy.

6. **[D] Canonical exhaustiveness can suppress curiosity.** French philosophical training encloses successful students in a tiny set of great texts. Mastery of the canon then operates as a bulwark against ideas from elsewhere. (p. 18)

7. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] A discipline can exclude societies through its evidence rules.** Detienne describes classifications that treat societies as lacking civilization, writing, or history while privileging national archives and written documents. (pp. 10–12, 40–56) **[I | INFERENCE]** Within that scoped classification system, the preferred evidence helps produce the recorded absence. This is not a claim that every evidentiary absence is institutionally caused.

8. **[D] Official scientific status and epistemic independence can diverge.** Soviet history can brand itself scientific while submitting to ideological rewriting. Chinese and Japanese historiography can be sophisticated and centrally commissioned. Roman priestly office can combine temporal expertise with ritual authority. (pp. 40, 48–50, 56)

9. **[D] Credential conversion can transform a social role.** Under colonial demands for written history, Mossi griots become the people deemed best qualified for the imported historian position. The credential does more than recognize an existing role; it restructures the role and the identity work expected of it. (p. 51)

10. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Detienne links privileged scholarly objects to institutions of training and recognition.** His examples include Athens, Greece, Thucydides, Homer, the Nation, and major Olympian gods. (pp. 7–18, 47, 53–65, 79–80) **[I | INFERENCE]** This supports testing a feedback relation in which prestige guides object choice and repeated object choice later appears to confirm natural centrality.

11. **[D] Interdisciplinarity can reproduce service hierarchy.** Asking how anthropology is useful to ancient history leaves ancient history as the owner and anthropology as a supplier. Detienne’s collaborative alternative gives both sides reciprocal question-changing power. (pp. 19, 36–37)

12. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Detienne describes disciplinary crisis producing defensive returns to masters, methods, sources, terrain, and credential control in his cases.** (pp. 16–18) **[I | INFERENCE]** This makes crisis a useful occasion to test for gate hardening. It does not establish a universal or predictable response in every field.

13. **[D] Detienne’s positive model depends on expertise.** Philology, fieldwork, archaeology, archives, inscriptions, calendars, deep linguistic knowledge, ritual knowledge, and historical reconstruction remain indispensable. His critique targets monopoly, framing, hierarchy, and veto power. (pp. 19–24, 60–78)

14. **[D] Expertise becomes collaborative competence when it remains corrigible.** Each participant retains close knowledge of a cultural system and absorbs the other participants’ questions. The group’s competence develops through regular contact rather than title alone. (p. 24)

15. **[P] The Polymyth credentialism definition remains precise:** title, office, degree, or rank substitutes for tested warrant. Detienne supplies direct evidence for academic versions of that substitution when credentials authorize object ownership, comparison vetoes, and approved problem sets. He also supplies direct evidence for real expertise that should survive the critique.

16. **[P] Add a jurisdiction audit to credentialism analysis.** Ask which claim the credential actually warrants, which further jurisdiction is being asserted, who can challenge it, what evidence would defeat it, and whether institutional rank is carrying weight that the evidence cannot.

17. **[P] Add an object-ownership audit.** When a specialist says a comparison is illegitimate, distinguish evidence-based limits from territorial veto. Require the specific source error, mistranslation, scale mismatch, chronology problem, or category contamination that defeats the comparison.

18. **[P] Add a credential/competence/authority matrix.** Credentials, demonstrated competence, institutional authority, public trust, evidentiary warrant, and accountability are separate axes. Detienne’s cases show every possible mismatch among them.

19. **[P] Add an incentive-sensitive unit check.** Ask whether the field’s standard unit—nation, monograph, great text, boss-god, period, archive, diagnosis, or individual author—reflects the object’s relations or the institution’s training and career structure.

20. **[P] Add a counterinstitution question.** Detienne’s constructive spaces include small centers, cross-disciplinary networks, regular microgroups, and scholarship conducted away from rigid university divisions. Critique becomes stronger when it specifies the arrangement that could support competence without property rights over thought.

21. **[T] The book does not support an anti-academic or anti-expertise conclusion.** It supports a distinction between scholarship and scholastic enclosure, peer correction and peer jurisdiction, documentary rigor and archive fetish, local competence and proprietary terrain, qualifications and automatic authority.

22. **[T] Detienne’s polemics sometimes compress opponents.** His portrayals of historians, philosophers, Hellenists, monographers, archaeologists, and national traditions often require direct reading of the criticized sources before they can support person-level motive claims or comprehensive field histories.

### Boundaries

1. **[D] Detienne uses boundary language across several distinct layers.** These include spatial limits, territorial borders, centers and edges, disciplinary jurisdictions, civilizational exclusions, evidentiary thresholds, membership gates, speech rights, divine fields of action, analytic scopes, national inheritances, and temporal separations. The layers must remain typed.

2. **[D] A boundary has a genesis.** In the foundation inquiry, the analyst asks whether a boundary comes from bush, village, earth surface, political decision, ritual act, movement, local power, or an arriving founder. (p. 28)

3. **[D] A boundary has a material and ritual form.** It may be cut, traced, encircled, paced, walled, planted, purified, spoken, allotted, inscribed, or left unmarked. It may take the form of a threshold, window, hinge, bolt, center, hearth, stone circle, precinct, road, or moving wake. (pp. 27–35, 72–77, 84–96)

4. **[D] A boundary has a permeability regime.** It can be fixed, shifting, porous, inviolable, passable, periodically renewed, temporarily generated, or erased. (pp. 26–31)

5. **[D] A boundary has an enforcer.** Detienne explicitly asks who or what makes it observed. The enforcer may be a founder, local Earth-power, god, official, priest, court, professional corporation, canon, ritual, law, architecture, weapon, document, or peer body. (p. 28 and throughout)

6. **[D] A boundary produces positions.** It creates inside/outside, center/periphery, member/nonmember, speaker/listener, citizen/resident foreigner, official/unrecognized witness, canonical/noncanonical object, civilized/uncivilized society, historian/ethnological object, and major/accessory deity. (pp. 9–18, 34–35, 40–56, 57–78, 79–99)

7. **[D] Boundaries can exist at a center.** Hearths, altars, speaking centers, central tables, thrones, rostra, and stone circles organize access and authority. A boundary is therefore an interface or ordering relation rather than only an outer perimeter. (pp. 28, 84–96)

8. **[D] Boundaries can be enacted by movement.** A moving body can create and dissolve borders. Apollo’s feet, founder paths, processions, and temporary Vedic precincts show that spatial order can arise through action rather than permanent enclosure. (pp. 27–29, 72–77)

9. **[D] Boundary claims can manufacture the identity they claim to recognize.** Athenian autochthony, descent rules, national history, and canon custody present a pure identity as ancient while laws and institutions actively produce it. (pp. 33–36)

10. **[D] Internal equality can coexist with an exclusionary membership boundary.** The Homeric assembly distributes speech among armed men. Cossack equality operates inside a warrior community. Ochollo participation changes by age and gender over time. Equal speech inside a circle says nothing by itself about who enters the circle. (pp. 82–98)

11. **[D] Architecture can encode a prior authority regime.** The 1789 hall was designed for listening to a king, not reciprocal deliberation. The National Assembly had to redesign agenda, speaking position, sightlines, acoustics, committees, and procedure to make its sovereignty operable. (pp. 85–86)

12. **[D] Publicity is a boundary operation.** Notaries, scribes, messengers, inscriptions, recitation, and vocalization determine who can know a decision, how law leaves the meeting, and where public authority reaches. (pp. 92–95)

13. **[D] Divine domains are overlapping boundaries.** A god’s field of action is rarely exclusive. War, marriage, navigation, metallurgy, foundation, and thresholds are shared among powers whose roles differ by configuration. (pp. 61–78)

14. **[D] Analytic boundaries need challenge.** A comparison must narrow variables to gain resolution, then restore the larger configuration and ask whether the chosen scope created the result. Detienne demonstrates the value of narrowing and repeatedly exposes its risks. (pp. 69–78)

15. **[P] A Polymyth boundary record should contain:** boundary type; source; marker; act of production; location; permeability; authorized crosser; excluded party; enforcer; sanction; temporal duration; center/edge relation; claimed justification; material effects; contestation; and change history.

16. **[P] Boundary language should be translated into operations.** “Sets a boundary” is too general for analysis. Record whether the act limits time, access, speech, contact, responsibility, resource use, interpretation, evidence, membership, or jurisdiction.

17. **[P] Distinguish a limit from a cutoff.** A specific limit can preserve relation under declared terms. A cutoff terminates or disables relation. The two may overlap in practice, and the result depends on the exact act, duration, enforcement, and recourse.

18. **[P] Distinguish self-protection from jurisdictional immunity.** A boundary can constrain what another person may do to the speaker. It can also be invoked to block any external claim, criticism, reciprocity, or accountability. The operative difference lies in which obligations and review mechanisms survive.

19. **[P] Ask whether the boundary is reciprocal.** Who receives protection, who owes duties, and whether both sides may make claims are empirical questions. In-group ethical gating should be shown through the distribution of protections and obligations rather than inferred from the word boundary alone.

20. **[P] Ask whether the boundary creates its own evidence.** Academic exclusion, court evidence rules, platform deletion, nonentry, silence, departure, and cutoff can shape the observed population. Any later metric drawn from the remaining participants needs a controlled-archive audit.

21. **[U] Current ML* already limits the project’s therapeutic-boundaries entry.** It separates ancient in-group-law analogy from modern genealogy and requires specific evidence for modern normalization, professional recommendation, moral legitimation, and command vocabulary.

22. **[D | SOURCE-NONCOVERAGE] Detienne supplies a boundary-analysis method rather than a study of modern therapeutic discourse.** The book does not establish DSM-era chronology, prevalence of cutoff, professional recommendations, interpersonal motives, or a continuous genealogy from ancient civic membership to contemporary therapy language. **[U | PROJECT-CURRENT]** This noncoverage does not oppose the user's claim as presented.

23. **[P] The legitimate Polymyth use is methodological:** apply Detienne’s questions to modern boundary discourse and collect modern evidence separately. This preserves the current historical-scope control.

### Historicity, memory, myth, and archives

1. **[D] Existing through time differs from recognizing historicity.** A society may undergo change without representing change as history. (pp. 42–47)

2. **[D] Memory differs from a represented past.** Memory can ground covenant, conduct, ancestry, identity, ritual, or continuity without creating an autonomous past object for inquiry. (pp. 43–50)

3. **[D] Change differs from progress.** Complex theories of change do not imply an infinite developmental sequence. (pp. 46–47)

4. **[D] Future orientation differs from linear time.** Roman destiny and futurity can intensify within temporal arrangements unlike modern chronology. (pp. 49–50)

5. **[D] Event singularity is one temporal regime.** Modern historicism prizes contingent novelty. Chinese annalistic arrangements can treat events as signs within moral-cosmic order. These are different operations of event meaning. (pp. 47–49)

6. **[D] An artifact’s modern evidentiary use differs from its original function.** A funerary stele may be a foundational document for a modern historian without having been produced as historiography. (p. 51)

7. **[D] Writing differs from criticism.** Ritual comparison, oral counter-story, variant placement, semantic choice, and performance can all support reflexive work. (pp. 46, 55–56)

8. **[D] Variant plurality can coexist with central control.** Japanese court philology preserves variants side by side while operating under imperial commission. Textual plurality supplies no automatic guarantee of political autonomy. (p. 56)

9. **[D] One culture can host several regimes.** Detienne explicitly rejects a necessary one-to-one mapping between culture and historicity regime. (p. 43)

10. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Documentary abundance is institutionally uneven in Detienne’s cases.** Courts, states, priesthoods, palaces, colonial administrations, scripts, and academic disciplines participate in producing and preserving unequal records. (pp. 40–56) **[I | INFERENCE]** This supports an archive-production audit in those scoped cases. It does not prove that power alone caused every surviving archive or every difference in evidentiary abundance.

11. **[P] A Polymyth historicity profile should ask:** how past, present, and future relate; who narrates; what counts as evidence; which medium carries the account; how variants are handled; what institution authorizes it; what change means; whether ancestors or gods remain present; and what future the account authorizes.

12. **[P] Keep semantic coherence, social function, historical accuracy, and institutional authority on separate axes.** A regime can be internally coherent and politically effective while its factual claims remain false or unverified.

13. **[P] Track retrospective conversion.** Modern disciplines often turn ritual, epic, monument, myth, genealogy, and inscription into evidence for purposes different from those of their makers. Record the conversion instead of projecting the modern category backward.

14. **[T] Detienne’s anti-hierarchy remains incomplete.** He rejects societies-with-history versus societies-without-history, then repeatedly treats autonomous interest in a past-in-itself as rare and difficult. The chapter never fully settles whether this difference is descriptive or quietly developmental.

15. **[T] Large labels compress internal plurality.** China, India, Rome, Israel, Greece, Japan, and Africa sometimes span long periods and heterogeneous institutions. The chapter’s own one-culture/one-regime warning should be applied to its examples.

### Polytheism, figures, and Polycognates

1. **[D] A deity is positional.** Detienne’s primary unit is a configuration of powers joined by association, opposition, complementarity, hierarchy, overlap, and situational grouping. (pp. 59–60)

2. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] Detienne begins from source-attested relations.** Shared altars, multi-deity sanctuaries, festivals, ritual sequences, pairs, triads, and lists offer evidence already organized relationally. (p. 60) **[P | PROPOSAL]** Making this an unconditional project-wide priority would require user ratification; this note does not ratify it.

3. **[D] Field and mode are useful, then dangerous.** Asking where a deity acts and how it acts improves analysis. Requiring one constant deity-specific mode can restore the isolated essence the relational method displaced. (pp. 61–64)

4. **[D] Domains are crossed and shared.** Marriage and war contain several powers, and a deity’s absence from one scene can be configuration-specific. (pp. 62–63)

5. **[D] Concrete probes discriminate.** Horse, bit, foot, stone, threshold, road, altar, and other narrow objects expose relations hidden by broad personality labels. (pp. 65–77)

6. **[D | SOURCE-DIRECT] The Greek divine figures analyzed here are plural within constraints.** They retain visual forms, stories, local identities, and histories while occupying variable relational positions. (pp. 77–78) **[I | INFERENCE]** The chapter supports this scoped statement about the Greek cases it treats. It does not establish that every figure in every narrative or tradition is plural in the same way.

7. **[P] For Polycognates, corresponding structural position should be reconstructed from relations.** Shared appearance, name, domain, or atmosphere supplies a candidate. A strong case identifies each figure’s system, position, neighboring figures, objects, acts, limits, and consequences.

8. **[P] Preserve a counterconfiguration.** If a figure is described as a threshold power, actively search for scenes where it appears without threshold work and for other figures that perform threshold work. This tests distinctiveness.

9. **[P] A Polycognate can be local.** A relation may hold only in one work, period, ritual, city, variant, or scene. Local correspondence is more precise than an oeuvre-wide or tradition-wide identity claim.

10. **[P] Restore personal and historical specificity after the positional pass.** Detienne brackets individual characteristics provisionally. Polymyth needs an explicit return-to-source step so that figures never become interchangeable nodes.

11. **[P] Separate grouping scale from figure scale.** A pair, triad, ritual host, narrative ensemble, institutional calendar, and whole pantheon may produce different relations. Membership in the same set says little until arrangement is specified.

12. **[T] The chapter demonstrates the method mainly with Greek material.** Its final invitation to compare broader polytheisms is a research program. Cross-cultural generalization remains to be earned.

### Assemblies, politics, publicity, and Seminar design

1. **[D] Political beginnings are plural.** Greece, medieval Italian communes, Cossack communities, Ochollo assemblies, and the French Revolution show separate configurations of collective deliberation. (pp. 79–99)

2. **[D] Begin with practices.** Ask who convenes, enters, speaks, proposes, deliberates, decides, records, publicizes, enforces, and represents; where bodies sit or stand; which objects authorize speech; and which matters count as common. (pp. 81–99)

3. **[D] Practice can create the collective.** Recurrent assembly, shared property, war booty, land allocation, speech rules, and publicity progressively form a community and its common affairs. (pp. 82–96)

4. **[D] Space is political infrastructure.** Circles, semicircles, central altars, thrones, rostra, standing stones, reserved agoras, and acoustics distribute visibility, audibility, status, and intervention. (pp. 83–93)

5. **[D] Equality is plural.** Equal shares, equal speech, legal equality, resemblance, interchangeability, consent, consensus, majority rule, and minority protection are distinct constructions. (pp. 96–98)

6. **[D] Publicity makes decisions public.** Writing, recitation, messengers, notaries, scribes, inscriptions, and speaking law extend an assembly beyond the moment of meeting. (pp. 92–95)

7. **[D] Democracy is never established by a circle alone.** The circle’s membership, speaking order, agenda, office arrangement, decision rule, enforcement, and publicity determine the political form. (pp. 84–98)

8. **[P] Seminar Schools can be audited as assemblies.** Record who selects the corpus, who may speak, how turns are distributed, what objects confer authority, how disagreement is retained, how decisions become public, how absent members are represented, and which practices make the group common.

9. **[P] Material layout belongs in pedagogy.** A room designed for one-way authority can sabotage a declared collaborative seminar. Sightlines, audibility, table shape, speaker position, notes, shared displays, and procedure need alignment with the pedagogical claim.

10. **[P] Curriculum publicity matters.** A group’s conclusions, open questions, dissent, source record, and changes need a form that survives the session. Minutes and shared notes are political-pedagogical infrastructure rather than clerical residue.

11. **[P] Preserve participation boundaries as historical variables.** Age, role, gender, expertise, preparation, attendance, and facilitation can change. A seminar should record the reason and review path for each eligibility rule.

12. **[P] Measure practice rather than branding.** Calling a group democratic, egalitarian, Socratic, collaborative, or student-led establishes no result. Observe turn distribution, agenda power, interruption, proposal uptake, dissent survival, and decision procedure.

## A Detienne-derived comparison worksheet

This is a provisional extraction for project use. It is not a quotation, Detienne protocol, or ratified ML* rule.

### A. Bound the material

1. Exact source, edition, version, period, place, and manifestation.
2. Included and excluded material.
3. Evidence classes available: text, image, performance, ritual, object, archive, oral account, built space, institutional procedure.
4. Missing, mediated, translated, reconstructed, or disputed evidence.

### B. Reconstruct each system independently

5. Local vocabulary and category lineage.
6. Relevant actors, figures, objects, gestures, spaces, institutions, and sequences.
7. Attested relations among them.
8. Internal variants, historical layers, conflicts, and unresolved ambiguities.
9. The source’s own evidence and authority regime.
10. The analyst’s dependence on disciplinary or institutional mediation.

### C. Declare the comparison

11. Exact comparative question.
12. Initial category and its expected productive range.
13. Axis or concrete probe.
14. Proposed relation class: correspondence, compatibility, analogy, structural homology, genealogy, causal equivalence, independent convergence, surface resemblance, or another declared type.
15. Scale: object, gesture, scene, figure-position, microconfiguration, institution, corpus, or historical system.
16. Known transmission or source-contamination routes.

### D. Construct the comparable

17. Elements retained from each case.
18. Relations among those elements.
19. Shared orientation or mechanism.
20. Load-bearing differences.
21. Constraints that keep the configuration from being arbitrary.
22. Alternative axis producing a different grouping.

### E. Run the negative cases

23. Case that resists the category.
24. Expected feature absent or transformed.
25. New variable exposed by resistance.
26. Category revision: split, narrow, expand, rename, or abandon.
27. Negative control that should remain outside the proposed pattern.

### F. Challenge the boundary

28. Who drew the object boundary and for what purpose?
29. What happens when the scope expands or contracts?
30. Which constitutive material was bracketed?
31. Who or what falls outside the comparison?
32. Does the result depend on disciplinary ownership, archival abundance, canonical prestige, or professional convenience?

### G. Test and return

33. Further configuration that could confirm the proposed relation.
34. Further configuration that could qualify or reject it.
35. Return-to-specificity pass restoring history, language, locality, and figure individuality.
36. Null or indeterminate outcome retained where warranted.
37. Exact interpretive gain.
38. What the comparison leaves unsynthesized.
39. What external evidence is needed for causal, genealogical, prevalence, or truth claims.
40. Stopping rule and remaining validation debt.

## Strong objections and limitations to preserve

1. **[T] Disciplinary caricature.** Detienne’s history/anthropology opposition is sharper than his own evidence. Historians can compare; ethnologists can guard terrain; anthropology has its own colonial and evolutionist history.

2. **[T] Comparison can dominate.** Lafitau, Fontenelle, evolutionism, Barrès, and colonial experiments show that comparison can Christianize, infantilize, racialize, rank, and injure. Breadth supplies no automatic emancipation.

3. **[T] Selection power remains underformalized.** Analysts choose cultures, axes, litmus objects, variables, and stopping points. A desired result can be manufactured unless the choices are prospectively declared and challenged.

4. **[T] The experimental metaphor has limits.** Historical cases cannot be repeated, variables cannot be isolated completely, and archives are asymmetric. Detienne acknowledges nonrepeatability by preferring manipulation.

5. **[T] Bracketing can flatten.** Setting aside individual divine traits or chronology may reveal a relation and may also erase language, history, locality, causality, and devotion. The return pass needs explicit enforcement.

6. **[T] Anti-typology can become a latent typology.** Recurrent variables, microconfigurations, and orientations may solidify into types even when the method disavows classification.

7. **[T] Greek recentering persists.** Greece becomes an operator instead of a norm, yet the book repeatedly returns to Greek material and grants it disproportionate analytic density.

8. **[T] Myth-to-reason residue.** The book critiques Greek reason as evolutionary summit while sometimes describing myth as fading into legal, political, ethical, monetary, and philosophical rationality.

9. **[T] Large civilizational units.** China, Japan, India, Greece, Rome, Israel, Africa, and the West can conceal internal conflict, temporal change, class, locality, and alternative regimes.

10. **[T] Archival asymmetry.** Court, state, script, conquest, collection, translation, and academic selection determine what survives. Comparison needs an explicit missingness and mediation record.

11. **[T] Native authority is internally complex.** Treating theologians as authoritative native statements raises questions about which speakers count, whose practice they represent, and how explicit doctrine relates to material and popular configurations.

12. **[T] Political practice can hide coercion.** Beginning with assembly practice makes emergence visible and can understate inherited status, violence, exclusion, labor, and force.

13. **[T] Equality within a group can intensify exclusion outside it.** Warrior equality, citizen speech, property shares, and majority decisions need a membership and consequence audit.

14. **[T] Independent invention claims require transmission research.** Ochollo, Cossack, Greek, French, Japanese, Vedic, Chinese, and other configurations may be reasonably treated as separate for an analytic experiment while historical independence remains a distinct empirical question.

15. **[T] Some source chains are thin.** Chapter 5 has one endnote for a large comparative sweep. The detailed Delphi section relies largely on a blanket referral to Detienne’s earlier Apollo book. Several notes identify unpublished, incomplete, duplicated, anomalous, or indirectly cited sources.

16. **[T] No stopping rule.** Detienne’s open-ended journeys and recursive manipulations protect discovery while leaving saturation and completion undefined.

17. **[T] Ethical aim and method need a bridge.** Perspective on one’s values may encourage pluralism, yet comparison alone cannot determine how to live with others or resolve political conflict.

18. **[T] Origin critique can overreach.** Exposing nationalist or institutional origins of a method does not prove every later use invalid. Current practice must be tested in its own form.

## Bottom-line synthesis

Detienne is most useful to Polymyth as a discipline for constructing comparisons under conditions of radical difference. His method does four things especially well: it turns the resistant case into evidence, shifts attention from named themes to relational microconfigurations, exposes the institutions that police comparability, and makes boundaries answerable to their production and enforcement.

His academic critique supports a precise attack on credentialism: qualification becomes credentialism when title, office, degree, peer body, or disciplinary custody substitutes for tested warrant and expands into jurisdiction over what may be asked. The same book makes real competence indispensable, which blocks any easy anti-expertise reading.

His boundary work is methodologically rich and historically limited. It supplies a powerful grammar for spatial, institutional, evidentiary, political, divine, and analytic boundaries. It supplies no genealogy or prevalence evidence for contemporary therapeutic boundary discourse. That noncoverage does not oppose the user's claim as presented because the claim did not require such a genealogy. Polymyth can use the grammar immediately while keeping modern historical claims in their own evidence lane.

The newest project versions have already replaced the older lack-of-coordination shortcut with correspondence, compatibility, translatability, source contamination, typed relations, and transmission checks. The inherited sentence is therefore provenance for a resolved correction rather than a current contradiction. Separately, the project's four-register state already compresses gesture → ritual → idiom → narrative and preserves narrative → idiom → ritual → gesture as reverse activation. Detienne supports the corrected practice of reconstructing cases, declaring the comparative axis, testing transmission, identifying a relational configuration, preserving differences, running resistant cases, and allowing a null result.

Most importantly, **[U | PROJECT-CURRENT] Detienne is a point in favor of the always-already claim.** His comparative procedure can make an unnoticed relation intelligible without implying that the act of comparison created the relation's prior operation. The support is scoped and answerable. It does not turn Detienne into a Polymyth author, and it does not give every proposed pairing a free pass.
